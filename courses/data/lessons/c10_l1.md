A bat and a ball cost $1.10 together. The bat costs a dollar more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?

An answer has already appeared in your mind: ten cents. It arrived instantly, effortlessly, and with a pleasant feeling of confidence — and it is wrong. If the ball cost ten cents, the bat would cost $1.10, and together they would cost $1.20. The correct answer is five cents, and to get it you had to do something that felt entirely different from the first answer's arrival: you had to stop, frown slightly, and *work*. Most people don't. When Shane Frederick put this question to students at Harvard, MIT, and Princeton, more than half gave the intuitive wrong answer.[^1]

That little puzzle contains the single most important fact you will ever learn about the people you are trying to persuade, and it is the foundation on which this entire course is built: there are two ways of thinking inside every human being, they operate by completely different rules, and the one that actually makes most decisions is not the one your customer believes is in charge.

## The Two Systems

The psychologist Daniel Kahneman — who won the Nobel Prize in economics without ever taking an economics course, precisely for showing economists how people actually decide — popularized the names *System 1* and *System 2* for these two modes of thought.

System 1 is fast, automatic, effortless, and always on. It reads the emotion on a face in a tenth of a second, finishes the phrase "bread and...", flinches at a sudden noise, forms an impression of a stranger before he has finished his first sentence, and produces "ten cents" without being asked. It runs on association, pattern, feeling, and memory. It cannot be switched off — you cannot look at a word in your native language and choose not to read it — and it does the overwhelming majority of your mental life's work.

System 2 is slow, deliberate, effortful, and — this is the crucial part — *lazy*. It is what you use to compute 17 × 24, fill out a tax form, compare two insurance policies line by line, or check whether "ten cents" is actually right. It can do things System 1 cannot: follow rules, compare options on multiple attributes, reason from premises. But every minute of System 2 work is expensive. It burns attention and energy, it tires, and it will not engage unless something forces it to. Its default move, when System 1 offers an answer, is to wave it through.

Kahneman's summary of a lifetime of research is blunt: System 1 is the hero of the story. We think of ourselves as System 2 — the reasoning self who has beliefs, weighs options, and chooses deliberately. But most of the time System 2 is not the driver; it is the press secretary. System 1 generates the impression, the inclination, the feeling — and System 2, if it engages at all, engages mainly to compose the justification.

## What This Means for a Buyer

Now put a buyer in front of you — a customer weighing your product, a committee hearing your proposal, a spouse listening to your plan. The picture most sellers carry into that room is a System 2 picture: the buyer will gather the facts, weigh the pros and cons, compare the alternatives, and select the best option. So the seller brings facts, pros, comparisons — a spreadsheet argument aimed at the reasoning self.

But the reasoning self is not who greets you. What greets you is System 1, and it has already begun deciding before you finish your first sentence. It has formed an impression of you (trustworthy? nervous? one of us?). It has reacted to the *feel* of your offer — whether it seems safe or risky, familiar or strange, simple or complicated. It has noticed whether your message was easy or hard to process, and — as we will see in the lesson on cognitive ease — it quietly treats *easy* as a sign of *true*. Long before any spreadsheet is opened, System 1 has produced a lean: toward you or away from you.

Then comes the part that fools sellers most. The buyer's System 2 activates — and instead of auditing that lean, it usually goes to work *defending* it. Psychologists call the courtroom version of this motivated reasoning; the everyday version is simply what you have done every time you wanted something first and assembled the reasons second. The buyer who has warmed to you will find your case sheet full of compelling points; the buyer whose System 1 has gone cold will find the same sheet full of red flags. The facts did not change. The verdict came first, and the reasoning was hired afterward as its lawyer.

Hence the formulation you should write above your desk: **the sale is made to System 1 and justified by System 2.** You are always selling to two audiences inside one skull — a fast, feeling, impression-forming audience that actually decides, and a slow, reasoning audience that must be given everything it needs to ratify the decision and defend it to others. Sellers fail in both directions. The spreadsheet-seller aims everything at System 2 and wonders why an airtight case produces no movement: he has won the lawyer and never met the judge. The pure-hype seller wins the judge and starves the lawyer: the buyer *wants* to say yes but cannot justify it to a boss, a spouse, or his own morning-after self — and the deal dies in committee, or gets returned.

## Selling to Both

The practical craft, then, has two halves, and the order matters.

*For System 1*, the currency is impression, story, and ease. Lead with the concrete and the vivid, not the abstract: one named customer whose Tuesday changed is worth a page of capability statements, because System 1 thinks in pictures and particulars. Make the message effortless — short sentences, plain words, one idea at a time — because friction reads as doubt. Mind everything that speaks before your content does: your warmth, your confidence, the simplicity of your materials, the first thirty seconds. And aim at feeling honestly: the relief of a problem gone, the pride of a good decision — the next lesson will show that this emotional register is not a manipulation of the decision but the very material decisions are made of.

*For System 2*, the currency is ammunition. Once the lean exists, the buyer needs to justify it — often literally, to other people. So build what you might call the justification packet: the clean one-page case, the numbers, the comparison, the answers to the three obvious objections, the references. Its job is not to create desire; desire is System 1's department. Its job is to make yes *defensible* — to arm your buyer to be your advocate in the rooms you will never enter. A good test of your own pitch: could your buyer, one week later, explain to a skeptical colleague why they chose you, in two sentences, without you there? If not, you have sold to the judge and left the lawyer with an empty briefcase.

## The Honest Counterpoint

Now the discipline this platform demands: the strongest case against what you have just read. Three objections deserve answers.

First: *doesn't this insult the buyer?* People are not idiots, and treating them as bundles of reflexes is both wrong and self-defeating. Answer: the two-systems model does not say people are irrational; it says reasoning is expensive and impressions come first — which is true of you, too, including on your smartest days. Respecting a buyer means working with how minds actually operate, not with a flattering fiction. And note that the model *raises* the bar for honesty: precisely because impressions are powerful, the seller who engineers a false one is doing real harm. Unit 9 draws that line hard.

Second: *don't high-stakes buyers engage System 2 for real?* Yes — and this matters enormously. Neil Rackham's research on large sales, which anchors Unit 6, found that techniques that work in small, quick sales actively backfire in large ones, exactly because bigger risk summons more deliberation, more stakeholders, more scrutiny. The two-audience principle still holds — the committee members each have a System 1, and "no one ever got fired for buying IBM" is a System 1 sentence — but as stakes rise, the justification packet stops being the closing argument and becomes the price of admission.

Third: *is the science solid?* Mostly, with honest caveats. The core dual-process picture and findings like the bat-and-ball problem are robust. But some of the flashier "priming" results reported in Kahneman's book failed to replicate, and Kahneman himself publicly conceded he had placed too much faith in them.[^2] Critics like Gerd Gigerenzer add that fast heuristics are often *smart* — well-adapted shortcuts, not defects. Take the caution seriously: this course will not build on the shaky studies. But the caution changes nothing about the operating rule, because Gigerenzer's point cuts the same way — whether System 1 is a bias machine or an adaptation machine, it is the machine that greets you first.

## The One Move

Take the most important pitch, proposal, or ask currently on your plate and split it into its two audiences. First, write the System 1 opening: one concrete, vivid story or picture of the after-state — a specific person, a specific day, the problem gone — told in plain words in under ninety seconds. Second, build the System 2 packet: one page, containing the numbers, the comparison to the alternative (including doing nothing), and honest answers to the three most likely objections — written so your buyer could forward it to their most skeptical colleague without you in the room. Lead with the first; leave the second. From now on, every pitch you make has both halves, in that order.
