In the 1980s, a patient the literature calls Elliot walked into the office of the neurologist Antonio Damasio with a strange complaint. Elliot had been a successful businessman, a good husband and father, a respected man — until a small tumor was removed from the underside of his brain's frontal lobes. The surgery saved his life and left his intelligence intact. Tested afterward, his IQ was superior. His memory was excellent. His logic was flawless; he could analyze a business problem as well as ever, list the options, and articulate the consequences of each. And yet his life was quietly disintegrating. He could no longer *decide*. Given a choice between two appointment dates, he could argue either side brilliantly — for half an hour — without ever choosing. He sorted documents all afternoon, unable to settle which task mattered. He lost his job, his marriage, his savings, and then a second marriage, one undecidable or badly decided choice at a time.

What the surgery had damaged was not Elliot's reasoning but his *feeling*. The region removed, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, is a junction where emotion is knitted into thought. Elliot could compute every option but no option *felt* like anything — and so nothing tipped. Damasio, who had inherited the old view that good decisions come from keeping emotion out of the room, drew the conclusion the evidence forced on him: reason alone cannot choose. Emotion is not the enemy of decision. It is the mechanism of it.[^1]

## The Machinery of a Choice

Damasio's account, the somatic marker hypothesis, runs like this. As you live, your brain tags experiences with feelings — bodily echoes of past outcomes, faint pulses of attraction and aversion. When you later face a choice, those markers fire before deliberate analysis gets anywhere: this option feels warm, that one tightens the stomach. The markers do the essential work that pure logic cannot do, which is to *rank* things — to make one future matter more than another. Reason can tell you that Option A yields twelve units of something and Option B yields ten; only feeling can tell you whether you care. Strip the feeling out, as Elliot's surgery did, and you get exactly what he became: a perfect analyst who cannot buy lunch.

Notice how precisely this fits the previous lesson. System 1's verdicts arrive *as feelings* — the lean toward or away that greets every offer before any spreadsheet opens. Damasio supplies the deeper claim: the feeling is not decoration on the decision. The feeling *is* the decision, in embryo. What we call "making up our mind" is very often the moment the feeling reaches threshold; what we call "our reasons" is very often the account System 2 writes afterward. Hence this lesson's title, which you should treat as the second law of persuasion: emotion decides, reason explains.

One caution before we use this. "Emotion" here does not mean melodrama — tears, terror, euphoria. It means the whole quiet register of valuation: comfort and unease, trust and wariness, pride, relief, ambition, the sense of safety, the fear of looking foolish. A procurement manager choosing between two vendors on a Tuesday afternoon is not weeping, but his decision is running on exactly this machinery: which choice feels safer, which vendor feels like trouble, which outcome he can already feel explaining to his boss. B2B buyers are not exempt from emotion; they simply feel different emotions — and fear of blame is the strongest of them, a thread Unit 4 picks up.

## What Buyers Are Actually Feeling

If feeling is the deciding vote, the seller's first job is not to present but to *locate*: which emotions are actually in the room? A short field guide, each of which later lessons will develop:

Fear is the heavyweight — fear of loss, of waste, of being cheated, and above all of making a mistake that others will see. In most stalled deals the obstacle is not preference for a rival but dread of deciding at all. Relief is fear's twin and one of the most underrated things you can sell: the exhale when a nagging problem is finally handled. Pride and status run silently under almost everything — the buyer imagining how this choice makes him look, to others and to himself. Ambition — the pull of the better future self — powers education, fitness, tools, and every vision ever cast. Belonging moves the church member, the voter, and the enterprise buyer alike: what do people like us choose? And trust, the master feeling, is the medium all the others travel through; wound it and no argument survives.

The craft implication is a reordering of the seller's work. Amateurs present features; professionals first ask their way to the feeling. Questions like "What happens if this doesn't get fixed this year?" or "What would it mean for you personally if this worked?" are not soft talk before the real conversation — they *are* the real conversation, because the answers tell you what the decision will actually run on. And when you present, aim the picture at the feeling you found: don't describe the backup software, describe the Friday the server died and nobody panicked. Let the buyer pre-experience the emotional payoff — for as Damasio's markers imply, a choice is a competition between imagined futures, and the future that is *felt* most vividly wins.

Then — the previous lesson's discipline — hand reason its brief. The feeling makes the decision; the reasons make it defensible. Your buyer chose with the heart and must now answer to a boss, a spouse, or his own inner auditor, all of whom speak System 2. The justification packet is how you make sure the feeling survives contact with the committee.

## The Honest Counterpoint

Three objections, taken at full strength.

First, the scientific one. The somatic marker hypothesis is influential, not settled. The Iowa Gambling Task, its flagship experiment, has drawn serious methodological criticism, and reviewers note the theory's central claims have proven hard to test cleanly.[^2] Honest accounting: what is rock-solid is the clinical picture — patients with ventromedial damage who reason well and decide disastrously, replicated well beyond Elliot — and the broad conclusion that emotion is integral to normal decision-making, which is now mainstream neuroscience. What remains debated is the specific bodily mechanism. This course builds only on the solid part.

Second, the moral one, and it deserves the most respect: *"aim at the feeling" is the manipulator's creed.* Fear, pride, and belonging are exactly the levers of the demagogue and the boiler-room closer. The answer is not to deny the power but to govern it, and the governing line is truth: the honest persuader evokes emotions that are *warranted* — real fear of a real cost, real pride in a genuinely good decision, vivid pictures of a future the product actually delivers. The manipulator manufactures the feeling that the facts do not support. Same machinery, opposite relationship to reality; Unit 9 makes the test precise. Note also that the emotional register, honestly used, is frequently the *more* truthful one: a spreadsheet that omits what a failing project is doing to a team's morale is not the objective version of the story — it is the incomplete one.

Third, the practical one: some buyers really do run the numbers — the engineer with a scoring matrix, the CFO with a model. True, and sell to that; giving an analytical buyer a rigorous case is meeting *his* System 1, which finds sloppiness repellent and rigor reassuring. But watch what happens when the matrix produces a tie, or an answer he dislikes: suddenly the weights get revisited. The feeling holds the veto even in the spreadsheet's kingdom.

## The One Move

Open your pipeline, your project list, or wherever your current asks live, and add one column: **the feeling**. For each deal or ask, write the primary emotion actually driving this person — named specifically, not generically: not "wants efficiency" but "afraid of another quarter explaining the backlog to the board"; not "interested in the vision" but "wants to be part of something growing again." If you cannot fill in the column for someone, that is your diagnosis — you don't yet know why they would buy — and your next conversation has one goal: ask the question that finds out. "If this problem were gone six months from now, what would that change for you?" Then listen, write down the feeling in their words, and aim everything that follows at it.

[^2]: The critical literature: Dunn, Dalgleish & Lawrence, "The somatic marker hypothesis: a critical evaluation," *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* 30 (2006), which questions both the IGT's design (e.g., whether "anticipatory" skin responses reflect explicit knowledge) and the hypothesis's testability; see also Maia & McClelland, *PNAS* 101 (2004) on participants' conscious knowledge in the IGT. The broader consensus that emotion is constitutive of valuation and choice — independent of the somatic mechanism — is standard in decision neuroscience (e.g., Lerner, Li, Valdesolo & Kassam, "Emotion and Decision Making," *Annual Review of Psychology* 66 [2015]). On fear of blame as the dominant B2B emotion and losing to "no decision": Matthew Dixon & Ted McKenna, *The JOLT Effect* (2022) — treated at Unit 4, Lesson 8.
