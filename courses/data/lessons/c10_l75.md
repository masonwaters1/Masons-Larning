In 1990, WordPerfect owned word processing the way the sun owns daylight — roughly half the market, the corporate standard, an army of trained secretaries who knew its function keys by heart, and a reputation for legendary customer support. Lotus 1-2-3 held the same throne in spreadsheets; it was, more than any other product, the reason businesses had bought PCs at all. Fifteen years later both were trivia questions. Nor were they unlucky exceptions. Siebel Systems ruled customer-relationship software in 2000 and was sold off by 2005. BlackBerry owned the corporate smartphone in 2007 and was a corpse by 2013. WebEx *was* video conferencing until, quite suddenly, Zoom was. Internet Explorer held ninety-plus percent of browsing and lost it to a newcomer. The graveyard of dominant software is large, well-documented, and — this is the point of the lesson — organized. The giants did not fall randomly. They fell in patterns, and every pattern is a door a challenger walked through. Learn the doors.[^1]

## Door One: The Platform Shift

WordPerfect and Lotus were not out-built; they were out-*positioned* by history. Both were masterpieces of the DOS era, and when computing moved to Windows in the early 1990s, both hesitated — WordPerfect bet on other platforms and shipped a poor Windows version late; Lotus dithered. Microsoft, who owned the new platform and built Word and Excel natively for it, simply inherited the future. The lesson generalizes far beyond operating systems: whenever the ground shifts — desktop to web, web to mobile, on-premise to cloud, single-player to collaborative — every incumbent's fortress from last lesson is built on the *old* ground. Its data formats, its trained users, its integrations, its habits: all anchored to the world that is ending. On the new ground, everyone starts even, and the incumbent often starts worse, because its revenue, org chart, and pride are sunk in the old way. Siebel's on-premise empire could not follow Salesforce into the browser without destroying its own license model. Adobe's desktop kingdom could not follow Figma into the multiplayer browser tab without cannibalizing itself — which is why, rather than beat Figma, Adobe tried to buy it for twenty billion dollars. The challenger's question: what ground is shifting under *my* incumbent, and am I native to the new ground while they are a tourist on it?

## Door Two: The Bottom-Up Invasion

BlackBerry was not defeated in the IT department it had spent a decade fortifying. It was defeated in employees' pockets. People bought iPhones as consumers, loved them, and dragged them into work until IT surrendered — the "bring your own device" wave. Slack ran the same maneuver against email and HipChat: no CIO decided to adopt Slack; some team just started using the free version, then three teams, then the company was standardized before procurement ever held a meeting. Zoom did it to WebEx — a free tier that simply *worked*, spreading meeting by meeting. The pattern: when the incumbent's fortress is strongest at the top of the organization (contracts, relationships, blame-fear all live with the decision-maker), the challenger goes *under* it, winning individual users who face none of those walls, until the organization's official choice is merely ratifying what its people already did. Ask of your own market: who inside the customer can adopt me without anyone's permission — a single coach, a single position group, a single assistant — and is my entry version free or cheap enough, and delightful enough, for them to do it?

## Door Three: The Neglected Flank

Clayton Christensen's famous disruption pattern is the third door: incumbents, serving their biggest and richest customers, drift upmarket — more features, more complexity, higher prices — and in doing so *overshoot* the ordinary customer, who is left paying for capability he doesn't use. The challenger enters at the bottom, "worse" on the spec sheet but cheaper and simpler, takes the customers the incumbent barely notices losing, and improves year by year until the flank attack has become a frontal one. Salesforce began with small businesses Siebel wouldn't return calls for. Every incumbent that raises prices and piles on enterprise features is manufacturing this flank — a growing population of customers who are overcharged and underserved, whose needs are simple, and whose loyalty is thinner than the incumbent believes. In a sports-video market, that flank has faces: the small school, the youth program, the sport the incumbent treats as an afterthought, the team using a tenth of the features at full price. The flank is where "more affordable and just as good" — weak as a frontal message, as the next lesson shows — becomes devastating, because for the overshot customer, "just as good" at the features he actually uses is the literal truth.

## Door Four: The Stumble

Finally, giants open the door themselves. They get acquired and lose focus (WordPerfect passed through Novell's hands mid-crisis; countless beloved tools have curdled after a private-equity purchase). They hike prices and remind every customer, on the same renewal date, to go shopping. They let support decay from legendary to ticket-queue. They insult a segment. The stumble rarely kills — the fortress absorbs the shock — but it does something precious for you: it converts satisfied customers into *lookers*. The next lesson's trigger events are largely a catalog of incumbent stumbles, and the challenger's job is to be already known, already trusted, and already easy to try on the day the stumble lands.

## The Honest Counterpoint

Survivorship warning: this lesson has shown you the giants who fell, and for each of them, a hundred challengers with the same doors available died unremembered. Incumbents usually win — Word and Excel have now held their thrones far longer than the products they overthrew, seeing off decades of cheaper, arguably-as-good rivals; Google's browser and Amazon's store shrug off challengers annually. And the fall, when it comes, is slower than the retold stories suggest: WordPerfect's collapse took most of a decade; Siebel fought Salesforce for six years; "sudden" Zoom was nine years old when the pandemic hit. The doors are real, but they are necessary rather than sufficient — walking through one still requires excellent execution, patient capital, and years. The sober use of this history is not "incumbents always fall" but "when incumbents fall, it is through these doors — so aim only at an incumbent standing near one, and pack supplies for a long siege."[^2]

## The One Move

Write the four-doors memo for your own fight — four short paragraphs. One: what platform or behavior shift is underway in my market, and how am I native to it? Two: who inside the customer can adopt me bottom-up with no permission, and what do I hand them free? Three: which segment does the incumbent overcharge and underserve, named specifically? Four: what stumbles has the incumbent made in the last two years, and which customers felt them? If all four paragraphs come up empty, that is a finding: you are planning a frontal assault on an unbreached wall, and your strategy — not your product — needs work before your marketing does.
