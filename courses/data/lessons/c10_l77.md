A customer who has renewed the same software nine years running is not, most of the time, choosing it. He is *not-choosing* — rolling forward a decision made long ago by someone possibly no longer there, protected by every wall of the fortress and by the simple fact that re-evaluating vendors is work nobody assigned him. Against a not-chooser, persuasion is nearly powerless; Unit 5 taught you that the great majority of any market is out-of-market at any moment, and the settled incumbent customer is the deepest form of out-of-market there is. But not-choosing is a spell, and spells break. Certain events force the settled customer to actually *look* at his software again — reference point shattered, System 2 summoned, vendors suddenly comparable — and in the window that follows, and roughly only then, a challenger gets a fair hearing. This lesson is a field guide to those cracks, and to the discipline of being present when they open.

## A Field Guide to Cracks

*The renewal shock.* The most reliable crack in all of software. An invoice arrives noticeably larger than last year's — the incumbent has raised prices, retired a tier, or unbundled something — and in one envelope, a not-chooser becomes a shopper. The genius of the renewal shock, from your side, is that the incumbent inflicts it on its whole customer base *on a schedule*: every account has a renewal date, and price increases sweep through in waves. Loss aversion, for once, fights for the challenger — the hike is coded as a loss from the old price, and the anger it generates is the emotional budget your pitch gets to spend. Corollary: the single most valuable piece of intelligence you can hold about any prospect is their renewal month, and the cheapest high-value question in your industry is "when does your Hudl deal come up, and what did it go up by?"

*The changing of the guard.* A new coach, a new athletic director, a new IT director, a new owner. The incumbent's relational wall — habit, mastery, the rep they've known for years — lived in the *predecessor's* head and left with him. The newcomer has no sunk costs, no muscle memory, often an active desire to make a mark, and a honeymoon in which change is expected rather than blamed. Research on B2B buying consistently finds leadership change among the strongest predictors of vendor change, and every seasoned enterprise seller watches announcements of new executives the way traders watch earnings.[^1] In a coaching market, the changing of the guard is public, seasonal, and listed — hiring announcements are literally published — which makes this the most systematically harvestable crack you have.

*The incumbent's stumble.* Last lesson's fourth door, seen from ground level: the botched rollout, the feature removed, the support ticket unanswered during the season, the outage on game night, the acquisition followed by a "streamlined" (worse) product, the beloved local rep replaced by a call center. Stumbles convert satisfaction into grievance, and grievance into shopping. You will rarely see the stumble directly; you hear it — in forums, at conferences, in the sigh a prospect gives when you ask, gently, "how's that been going for you?" Which is why challenger prospecting is mostly *listening posts*: being in the coaching Facebook groups, at the clinics, in the association meetings where the grumbling happens first.

*The budget quake.* Funding cut, enrollment down, a levy failed, a department merged — suddenly a line item that sailed through nine renewals is on a spreadsheet with a red cell next to it. Cost-cutting seasons are challenger seasons: the same price message that bounced off a comfortable buyer ("cheaper" — single-weighted gain) transforms for a squeezed one into "keep your film program *and* close the gap," a loss-prevention frame with double weight behind it.

*The requirement shift.* The org adds a sport, a level, a new use; a rule or format changes; a conference adopts a standard. Anything that makes the old tool newly *insufficient* reopens the question of tools — and the challenger who already serves the new requirement natively walks in as the specialist rather than the substitute.

## The Discipline: Present Before the Crack

Now the hard part, and the real content of this lesson. Cracks open on the buyer's schedule, not yours — which means the challenger's job between cracks is not persuasion but *positioning for the crack*. Three practices follow.

First, **build the trigger map**: a simple record, per prospect, of renewal month, contract length if known, staff tenure, and any grumbles heard — so that outreach is timed to windows instead of sprayed at walls. Second, **be already known when it opens**. Unit 5's mere-exposure lesson does its heaviest lifting here: the coach who has seen your name for a year — the useful newsletter, the free tool, the booth at the clinic, the teammate's mention — puts you on the shortlist reflexively the day the invoice stings. The unknown challenger, arriving cold into an open window, spends the whole window earning the trust the known challenger already banked. Third, **make the first step frictionless in advance**: the crack's window is weeks, not quarters, and it closes the moment the buyer grudgingly renews; if evaluating you requires a demo scheduled three weeks out, you will watch windows close forever. The instant trial, the pre-built comparison page, the "send us one game's film and we'll show you tomorrow" offer exist precisely so that an open window can be walked through the day it opens.

## The Honest Counterpoint

Two cautions. The first is ethical and simple: hunting cracks is legitimate; *manufacturing* them is not. Being present when the incumbent raises prices is fair game. Whispering exaggerated doom about the incumbent's acquisition, or frightening a new coach with invented horror stories, is the fear-mongering line Unit 1 drew — it poisons the tight community you sell into, and coaches talk. The second is strategic: trigger-chasing can curdle into passivity — a pipeline of "waiting for their renewal" is a pipeline of excuses. The evidence from Unit 5 cuts both ways: yes, buyers move when triggers hit, but the *winner* at trigger time is usually decided beforehand, by who built familiarity and trust during the boring years. Triggers determine *when* the door opens; the work you did before determines *who* is standing in it. The challenger who only shows up at renewal time is a discount flyer; the one who has been usefully present all along is the obvious alternative.[^2]

## The One Move

Build the trigger map this week. Take your top twenty-five prospects and create four columns: renewal month (ask, or note "unknown" — then make finding it the goal of your next touch), leadership tenure (new hire in the last year? — check the public announcements), known grievances (what have you actually heard?), and budget pressure (any public signal). Sort by nearest likely window. Your outreach calendar now writes itself: warm, useful, unhurried presence for everyone — and full attention, timed offers, and the frictionless first step aimed at whoever's window opens next.
