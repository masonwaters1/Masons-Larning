On June 6, 1944, the Allies did not attack the whole Atlantic coastline. They took five beaches — narrow strips chosen for where the defenses were weakest — and from that foothold rolled up a continent. The metaphor is worn shiny in business books, and it earned it, because it names the single most common strategic error challengers make: attacking the incumbent *everywhere*, with a general product for a general market, dispersing a small force across a defended coastline until it is thin enough to be ignored. The incumbent can be everywhere; that is what being the incumbent means. You cannot — and the counterintuitive discovery of this lesson is that your smallness, correctly aimed, is a weapon. This lesson turns the last four into a targeting doctrine: given the fortress (74), the doors (75), the wedge (76), and the cracks (77) — *where exactly do you attack first?*

## The Doctrine of the Narrow Front

Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm*, the most operationally useful book ever written for challengers, states the doctrine plainly: pick a beachhead segment small enough to dominate and win it *completely* — then expand to the adjacent segment, using the first as proof.[^1] Not "small schools" — a definable community whose members know each other, face the same situation, and talk: soccer programs in one state's small-school classifications; the lacrosse clubs of one metro; middle-school programs in three conferences. The reasons for narrowness compound. Focus lets a small team build the *exact* product, migration path, and message for one buyer type, out-serving the incumbent's one-size-for-everyone within the niche. Word of mouth, the challenger's only affordable channel, only ignites inside communities dense enough for references to travel — win 40% of a tight niche and every prospect in it already knows a happy customer; win 2% of everything and no prospect knows anyone. And dominance flips the herd: Unit 2's social proof normally guards the incumbent ("everyone uses them"), but inside a captured beachhead the sentence reverses — "everyone *in our conference* has moved" — and the fear of deciding wrongly starts working for you. The beachhead is where "people like us choose this" changes sides.

## Choosing the Beach

Score candidate segments against everything this unit has built. *Low walls* (74): little contract lock-in, light data history, minimal dependence on the incumbent's network — the program that never exchanges film through the platform and has no recruiting pipeline is standing outside Hudl's strongest wall. *Open doors* (75): the overshot flank — segments paying full price for a tenth of the features, or priced out entirely, or belonging to a sport the incumbent visibly treats as an afterthought — plus any bottom-up path where one coach can adopt you without a committee. *Wedge resonance* (76): the segment for whom your ten-times difference matters *most*. *Crack density* (77): where are renewals clustering, budgets tightening, new coaches arriving? And one more, from Moore: *reference connectivity* — do these buyers actually talk to each other? A segment that never assembles, compares, or gossips cannot amplify your wins. The ideal beachhead is underserved, lightly defended, wedge-hungry, budget-pressed, and *chatty*.

Notice what this scoring quietly forbids: chasing the flagship. The temptation to bag the famous program — the big district, the marquee name — is enormous, and usually fatal. The flagship sits behind every wall at maximum height: deepest data history, heaviest network use, richest incumbent attention, most blame-risk for its decider. You will spend a year of your small force's capacity losing to it, or worse, winning it and drowning in its demands. The flagship falls *last*, to the social proof of everyone around it — that is what land-and-expand means.

## Land, Then Expand

Which brings us to the second half of the doctrine, borrowed from how modern software actually grows: land small, expand from inside. The initial sale should be the smallest self-contained unit that can say yes on its own authority — one team, one coach, one season, one sport — because Unit 1 taught you the small yes is the easy yes, and Unit 2 taught you what happens after it: commitment and consistency go to work, and the customer who chose you begins defending the choice. Expansion then runs along two rails. *Inside the account:* the JV team's coach shows the varsity staff; one sport's success becomes the athletic director's pilot evidence; the single-team deal becomes, a season later, the department conversation — with your original coach, not you, making the pitch. *Across the community:* every won team is a reference node — the case study, the clinic testimonial, the coach who answers a peer's "is it actually good?" text. Your expansion motion, concretely, is asking happy customers two questions on a schedule: "who else on your staff should see this?" and "which coach in your conference should I talk to — and would you make the introduction?" In a market where the buyers referee each other's games on Friday nights, the referral rail is the whole railroad.

## The Honest Counterpoint

The doctrine has real costs; see them clearly. *Focus is risk*: choosing one beach means declining revenue from others while runway burns, and if the chosen segment is a dead end — too small, too poor, less connected than it looked — the error compounds before it is discovered. Moore's answer, ratified by experience: the risk of the narrow front is survivable and correctable (pick again), while the dispersed attack fails slowly, ambiguously, and completely. *Beachheads can become prisons*: companies over-fit to the first niche and never generalize — the segment-specific features, pricing, and brand that won the beach can repel the mainland. Schedule the expansion question deliberately ("what must be true before we open segment two?") rather than waiting for the beach to feel crowded. And a fair challenge from the other direction: Byron Sharp's brand-growth research argues that within *consumer* mass markets, growth comes from broad reach, not niche loyalty — a genuine tension worth knowing. The resolution is stage and structure: Sharp describes how large brands grow in low-consideration mass markets; Moore describes how small challengers enter high-consideration, referenced-based ones. A challenger displacing entrenched B2B software is squarely in Moore's world; you graduate to Sharp's reach logic *after* the beachheads have made you a name.[^2]

## The One Move

Choose your beach on paper. List five candidate segments, score each 1–5 on the five criteria — wall height (reversed), door openness, wedge resonance, crack density, chatter — and total them. Take the winner and write the beachhead sentence: "For the next two quarters, we sell to [exact segment], we lead with [wedge], our first offer is [smallest self-contained yes], and we win when [X of Y programs in the segment] are customers." Then enforce its sharpest edge: name the flagship-style prospect you are explicitly *not* pursuing this quarter, and don't.
