Everything in this unit so far has been about *where* and *when* to attack. This lesson is about the offer itself — and it begins by restating the challenger's situation with brutal clarity. Lesson 74 showed that the buyer is defended by real costs and real fears. Lesson 76 showed that those losses are weighted double against your gains. Put together, they yield the master principle of challenger deal design: **you are not selling your product; you are selling the transition.** The buyer already half-believes your product is fine. What he does not believe is that *the move* will be fine — that his data will survive, his staff will adapt, his season won't wobble, and his name won't be attached to a mess. So the challenger's offer must be engineered, piece by piece, to make the switch *safe* (the downside is yours, not his) and *small* (the first yes is tiny). The incumbent sells software. You sell a soft landing.

## Making It Safe: Take the Risk to Your Side of the Table

Lesson 3 of this course established risk reversal as worth double its face value, and nowhere does it earn that premium like a displacement sale. Walk the buyer's fears from the switching-cost audit and delete them one by one.

*The data fear* — "years of film and records are trapped in the old system" — is deleted by **done-for-you migration**: not "we have an import tool" (which hands the work, and thus the dread, back to him) but "send us your export, and your library will be waiting in your account, organized, before your staff logs in the first time." This is expensive for you and worth it: in mature switching markets, from banks to phone carriers to software, the challenger *always* ends up absorbing the transfer work, because the transfer is the sale.[^1]

*The competence fear* — "my staff will fumble through a season of relearning" — is deleted by **white-glove onboarding**: live setup, training scheduled around their calendar (before the season, not during it), and a named human they can call. Note what the named human also does: he deletes a piece of the *relational* wall by replacing the incumbent's familiar rep with a better one.

*The catastrophe fear* — "what if it fails on a Friday night?" — is deleted by **parallel running**: run us alongside the incumbent for the pilot period; cancel nothing until you've seen a real game week on our platform. Parallel running looks like it invites unflattering comparison. Invite it. Nothing builds a champion like a buyer who watched both systems side by side and chose you — and nothing you say about your product is worth a tenth of what he concludes himself.

*The blame fear* — the decider's career exposure — is deleted by making the exit trivially easy and loudly stated: month-to-month or single-season terms, a plain-language guarantee ("if you're not keeping us at season's end, you pay nothing / we'll even help you move back"), no auto-renewal traps. The guarantee's economics favor you lopsidedly — you price the risk across hundreds of accounts; he was pricing it alone at double weight — and its *signal* may matter more than its substance: an incumbent locks customers in with multi-year contracts; a challenger confident enough to make leaving easy is making an argument about product quality that no brochure can make.

## Making It Small: Shrink the First Yes

Safety handles the size of the downside; smallness handles the size of the decision. The full switch — every team, every sport, a signed multi-year — is a System 2 committee ordeal soaked in blame-risk. So don't ask for it. Design a **pilot** that is the smallest self-contained yes from last lesson: one team, one month or one tournament, their real film, free or nearly so, with white-glove treatment. The pilot converts the decision from "shall we abandon our platform of nine years?" (enormous, frightening, deferrable) to "shall we let one team try a thing for a month at no risk?" (small, safe, approvable today) — and Unit 1 taught you that the small yes is not merely easier but *generative*: the trial hands them ownership, and the endowment effect starts pulling your direction; commitment and consistency begin their quiet work; your product's daily presence builds the familiarity that was the incumbent's private moat. The pilot is not a smaller version of the sale. It is the machine that manufactures the sale.

Two disciplines keep pilots from becoming purgatory. First, **define success before it starts**, together, in writing: "if by the end of the month your staff has broken down three games and the position groups are using clips weekly, we'll talk about the season." An undefined pilot ends in "we're still evaluating" forever; a defined one ends in a decision. Second, **give the pilot an honest clock**: pilot pricing and terms expire when the pilot does. That is real scarcity, not manufactured — you cannot white-glove the world indefinitely — and it supplies the deadline that Unit 5 showed every drifting decision needs.

## The Honest Counterpoint

Every promise in this lesson is a check your operations must cash, and the accounting is sobering. Free migration, human onboarding, parallel-period support, and generous guarantees make your cost of acquiring a customer brutally high — quite possibly higher than the first season's revenue — and a challenger who offers all of it to everyone will die of generosity before the incumbent notices. Three controls make the doctrine solvent. *Aim it*: the full soft landing goes to beachhead accounts and reference-quality wins, not to every curious tire-kicker; the audit from Lesson 74 tells you where the investment pays. *Meter it by lifetime, not season*: a customer who stays five years and recruits three peers justifies an expensive landing; run the arithmetic so you know your actual payback period rather than feeling it. *Never promise past your capacity*: one botched white-glove migration in a chatty community costs more than ten never attempted — the fortress lesson's warning about validating the buyer's fears applies double when you volunteered for the work. And one caution from the other side of the table: guarantees and easy exits can be gamed by serial free-riders. Accept it as a cost of doing business — the evidence from every industry that has tried generous guarantees is that abusers are few and the trust dividend dwarfs them — but write terms that make abuse inconvenient, not terms that make honest buyers nervous.[^2]

## The One Move

Design your soft landing on one page, in two columns. Column one — *Safe*: your migration promise, your onboarding promise, your parallel-running offer, and your exit guarantee, each written as a single plain sentence a coach would repeat to his athletic director verbatim. Column two — *Small*: your pilot, defined — who, how long, what it costs (ideally nothing), the three success criteria you'll set together, and the honest deadline on pilot terms. Then price the whole landing per account — hours and dollars — so you know exactly what a beachhead win costs you, and put the one-pager in front of your next real prospect with a single question: "which of these would have to be true for you to try one team for one month?"

[^2]: The pilot/free-trial motion and its conversion mechanics (endowment via usage, commitment escalation, defined success criteria) are standard in SaaS sales operations literature; the psychological rails are Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler (1990) on endowment (this course U1L10) and Cialdini on commitment/consistency (U2L2). On generous guarantees' net economics and low abuse rates, the retail evidence is summarized in Hart, "The Power of Unconditional Service Guarantees," *Harvard Business Review* (July 1988), and subsequent service-operations research; the CAC/payback discipline for high-touch B2B landings is treated throughout SaaS unit-economics practice (LTV:CAC and payback-period analysis).
