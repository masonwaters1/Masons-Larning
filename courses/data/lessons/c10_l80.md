In 1962, Avis was a distant, money-losing second to Hertz in rent-a-cars, and its new ad agency proposed something no sane company had done: admit it. "Avis is only No. 2 in rent a cars. So why go with us? We try harder." The campaign became one of the most studied in advertising history — Avis's market share climbed for years — and it endures as the founding text of challenger communications because of what it understood: **the challenger cannot out-shout the incumbent, but it can out-position him, and the highest ground available is candor.** Everyone already knew Avis was No. 2. Saying it out loud converted the market's knowledge from a weakness into proof of honesty — and then attached to that honesty a claim ("we try harder") that the fat, comfortable leader could never credibly make. This lesson is about how a challenger talks: about the incumbent, about itself, and about the choice — because in a displacement fight, *what you say about Goliath* is the most delicate instrument you own.[^1]

## Rule One: Respect Before Reframe

The amateur challenger trashes the incumbent — and it backfires for reasons this course has already given you. Your prospect *chose* the incumbent, possibly for years; Unit 1's consistency principle means an attack on Hudl lands as an attack on his judgment, and he will defend his judgment by defending them. Worse, in a chatty market, trash-talk marks you as small and bitter — low-status behavior, and Unit 3's frame-control lesson taught you that status leaks into everything. The professional pattern is *respect, then reframe*: concede, sincerely, what the incumbent is genuinely good at — "Hudl built this category; the exchange network is real, and for a big program using all of it, it's a fine choice" — and *then* pivot the frame to the dimension where the game changes: "the question is what a program like yours is paying for that it never touches." The concession costs you nothing (the prospect already believes it), buys you the credibility of an honest broker (two-sided messages are consistently rated more trustworthy and are more persuasive with skeptical audiences — a finding as old as the World War II persuasion studies), and earns you the right to draw the comparison on *your* axis.[^2] Which is the real move: per Unit 3, you never fight on the incumbent's frame ("who has the most features and the biggest network?" — Goliath wins that frame forever). You install the challenger's frame: *what are you actually using, what are you actually paying, and who actually answers when you call?* Avis never claimed to be bigger. It changed the question to who tries harder.

## Rule Two: Contrast, Honestly

Once the frame is yours, sharpen it with contrast — the defining challenger genre. Apple's "Get a Mac" ads (2006–09) personified the choice: PC, a fussy, corporate, slightly broken bureaucrat; Mac, relaxed and competent — never naming a lie, just staging a contrast the viewer completed himself. Salesforce marched under an actual "no software" logo — the entire incumbent category crossed out — and threw mock protest rallies at Siebel's conference; the theater was outrageous, but the claim underneath ("no installation, no IT project") was true and verifiable. That is the discipline that separates challenger contrast from smear: every contrast you draw must be *specific, verifiable, and about the buyer's experience* — pricing published next to theirs, a two-column page a coach can check line by line, a side-by-side of what happens when each support line is called on a Friday. Comparison pages and "switching from X?" pages are among the highest-converting assets challengers own precisely because prospects in an open window are already comparing; your job is to make the comparison easy, honest, and framed on your axis. And keep one register above it all: make the *customer* the hero of every story, per Unit 7 — the ad is not "we beat Hudl"; it is "here's what the Jefferson County coaches did with the money they saved."

## Rule Three: Let Defectors Do the Talking

The challenger's most persuasive genre is the one you don't narrate: the testimony of the switched. Unit 2 taught that social proof runs on similarity — and no voice is more similar to a nervous prospect than someone who *was* that nervous prospect: same fortress, same fears, jumped anyway, lived. The switcher story has a natural arc that maps exactly onto your buyer's objections — *I doubted (name the fear), I tried (the pilot), the move was easier than I feared (the soft landing, confirmed), here's Friday night now (the after-picture)* — and its power is that the claims you most need made (the migration really was painless; the staff really did adapt in a week) are unbelievable from you and irresistible from a peer. So treat switcher stories as a production line, not a lucky break: capture one from every beachhead win, in the customer's own words, on video where possible, organized by segment so every prospect can find *his* mirror — the small-school AD hears a small-school AD, the lacrosse club hears a lacrosse club. In a market where buyers see each other at clinics and referee each other's games, the reference is the message; everything else you write is packaging around it.

## The Honest Counterpoint

Three cautions from the other side. First, the comparative tightrope is real: comparative claims invite scrutiny, and a single stretched line in your comparison table — one feature marked missing that ships next month, one price quoted stale — hands the incumbent a legitimate cudgel and converts your candor position into a credibility crater; comparative advertising is also legally policed (in the U.S., false comparative claims are actionable), so verify every cell as if Goliath's lawyers will read it, because they might. Second, naming the leader cuts both ways: every "vs. Hudl" page you publish also *advertises Hudl* and concedes the hierarchy — which is why the respect-then-reframe structure matters and why, as your beachheads mature, your communications should gradually shift from "the alternative to them" toward "the standard for us" (the segment you own), letting the comparison recede as your own name carries. Third, remember that Avis's honesty worked because the operations backed it — "we try harder" was, by contemporary accounts, drilled into the company as a service standard, not just a slogan — and a candor campaign atop mediocre delivery is the fastest self-immolation in marketing. The talk in this lesson only compounds on top of the substance in the last one.[^3]

## The One Move

Write your respect-then-reframe paragraph — the four sentences you will say, verbatim, every time a prospect mentions the incumbent: one sentence of sincere, specific credit; one sentence pivoting to your frame (usage, price, service — your axis); one sentence of verifiable contrast; one switcher's name they can call. Then build the asset behind it: a one-page, line-by-line honest comparison you would be comfortable having the incumbent's own team fact-check, and one recorded switcher story from your beachhead. Paragraph memorized, page published, story captured — that is your entire communications system for talking about Goliath, and it is enough.
