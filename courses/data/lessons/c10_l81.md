This unit closes where every campaign eventually arrives: the concrete offer on the table, and the incumbent's response when it realizes you are taking its customers. Everything so far has been aim — the walls (74), the doors (75), the wedge (76), the windows (77), the beach (78), the landing (79), the language (80). This lesson is the ammunition: the specific deal structures that move entrenched customers, the timing that makes them land, and the counterpunch you must expect the moment you start winning — because you will get exactly one campaign's worth of surprise, and then you are fighting a giant who knows your name.

## The Challenger's Offer Book

Each offer below is a targeted charge against one specific wall from Lesson 74; use them singly or stacked, always knowing which wall each is for.

**The contract buyout** — against the financial wall. The prospect loves you but has a year left on the incumbent's deal: credit the remaining term against your first year ("whatever you still owe them, you don't owe us"). It is the bluntest instrument in the book, standard practice in every switching market from phone carriers to payroll software, and its function is precise: it deletes the *only* wall protecting an otherwise-won account, converting "call me in a year" into "let's talk now." Meter it — buyouts go to beachhead and reference-quality accounts, priced against lifetime value, not sprayed at everyone.

**The price lock** — against the fear of you. The prospect's quiet suspicion about a cheap challenger is that the price is bait: once switched, you'll ratchet like the incumbent did. Kill the suspicion in writing: "your rate is locked for three years" or "your price never rises faster than X." The lock costs you little (you were not planning ambush pricing), and it does double duty — it removes a risk *and* it quietly indicts the incumbent's renewal-shock behavior without a word of trash talk. For a market that has been trained by years of surprise invoices, the lock is not a discount; it is a character reference.

**The bridge season** — against the timing wall. First season free or half-price *while they run out the incumbent's clock*: "start with us now; pay nothing until your Hudl term ends." This is the parallel-running offer of Lesson 79 weaponized into a deal: it moves the adoption date to *today* — familiarity, habit, and endowment start accruing on your side immediately — while letting the buyer feel he wasted nothing. When the incumbent's renewal notice arrives, it arrives at a customer who has already moved in with you.

**Champion enablement** — against the blame wall. Your coach still has to sell the athletic director, the board, the booster club — rooms you will never enter. So arm him, per Unit 1's justification packet: the one-page cost comparison with assumptions shown, the savings-per-season number in bold, the migration and guarantee promises in plain sentences, two peer references with phone numbers, and — the piece most challengers forget — *the risk answer written down*: what happens if it goes badly, in writing (the exit guarantee), so the decider can show his boss that the downside was covered. In displacement sales, deals are won or lost in the meeting you're not in; the packet is you, attending.

**The group flip** — against the network wall, and specific to markets like yours. Where the incumbent's power is the exchange between customers, the counter is to move customers *in sets*: the conference deal, the league package, the "bring your rivals" discount. If six programs that trade film with each other switch together, the network wall doesn't have to be climbed — it comes with them. This is the hardest offer to execute (it requires a champion with standing across programs, and patience) and the most devastating when it lands, because it converts the incumbent's best moat into a mass defection channel.

**Timing rule for all of it**: offers land in windows. The buyout and the bridge season are renewal-window plays; champion packets go out when the budget cycle starts, not when it ends; group flips are built in the off-season and closed before the fall. The trigger map from Lesson 77 is the calendar this offer book runs on.

## The Counterattack

Now the part challengers never plan for. For a while, you are too small to see. Then a sales rep loses three accounts in one conference, a district asks Hudl to match your price, and somewhere a spreadsheet acquires your name — and the incumbent's playbook opens. It is a short book, because incumbents everywhere run the same four plays. **The retention discount**: your prospect calls to cancel and is suddenly offered 30% off — the price that was impossible last month. Expect it, and pre-empt it by *naming it before it happens*: "when you call them, they'll suddenly find a discount — it's worth asking why that price only exists when you're leaving." This is inoculation, Unit 10's technique deployed early: the forewarned buyer experiences the retention offer not as generosity but as confirmation of your frame. **The FUD play** — fear, uncertainty, doubt, the incumbent's classic since IBM's mainframe days: they're tiny, they'll be gone in two years, who'll support you then, your data won't be safe. The answer is never outrage; it is the boring, factual confidence of Lesson 80 — customer count growing, references on the phone, guarantees in writing — plus the jujitsu question: "they may say we're too small to trust; ask them why their price needed us to exist before it came down." **The lock-in tightening**: longer contracts pushed at renewal, bundle discounts that make partial departure expensive, data export made subtly harder. You cannot stop this; you can make it visible — every tightening confirms, to a market you've primed, which company is confident and which is building walls. **The feature-match or acquisition**: they copy your wedge, or offer to buy you. The defense against the copy is Lesson 76's discipline — a true wedge is one they cannot match *without breaking something about themselves* (their price structure, their neglect of your segment, their support model); if your wedge was merely a feature, it was never a wedge. The acquisition offer is a chapter of its own life; just know the pattern — Adobe's twenty billion for Figma was the compliment an unanswerable challenger receives.

One more thing the counterattack does, and it is good news wearing armor: it validates you. In a chatty market, "Hudl is suddenly discounting against these guys" is the loudest advertisement you will never have to buy.

## The Honest Counterpoint

Two sobering audits. First, the economics of aggression: buyouts, free seasons, and price locks are all forms of paying customers to switch, and an incumbent with fifty times your revenue can absorb a price war you cannot — if the fight becomes purely monetary, you lose by arithmetic. The offer book only works *aimed*: at beachheads, in windows, backed by a wedge, with lifetime-value math done in advance — generosity as a rifle, never a firehose. Second, the ethics hold on your side of the line too: pre-empting the retention discount is fair; misrepresenting the incumbent's stability, terms, or intentions is the same FUD you condemn, and in a community where every coach knows three others, the challenger's only unrecoverable asset is the reputation for straight dealing. You are not merely trying to take accounts from Goliath. You are auditioning, in public, for the role of what this market's *next* trusted standard acts like — and every offer you write is evidence.[^1]

## The One Move

Build your offer card and your counterattack card — one page each. The offer card: the five structures above, each with your actual numbers filled in (what buyout you'll fund and for whom, the lock terms, the bridge structure, the packet contents, the group-flip target conference) and the window each deploys in. The counterattack card: the four incumbent plays, each with your two-sentence scripted response — including the inoculation line about retention discounts, which goes into every deal conversation from now on, *before* the buyer calls to cancel. Rehearse both cards until they're reflex; the campaign you've planned across this unit will meet reality at exactly these two moments — the offer, and the counterpunch — and the challenger who has already written his lines wins both.
