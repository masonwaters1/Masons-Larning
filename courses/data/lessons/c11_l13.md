Two units of theory have earned you this: the lesson where you actually sit down and do the thing. This is a complete walkthrough of breath-anchored meditation — the focused-attention practice from Lesson 4, the entry door of every tradition and every clinical protocol, the exercise whose network diagram you already own. Read it once through, then do it. Ten minutes. Everything in this lesson is calibrated to what those ten minutes will actually feel like, because the gap between what beginners expect and what happens is where most practices die in the first week — and you, unusually, have been inoculated: you know the mind will wander (L1), you know the wandering is the default network doing its job (L8), and you know the catch-and-return is the rep (a claim Unit 4 will earn in full). What's left is choreography.

## The Setup

**Where.** Anywhere you won't be interrupted for ten minutes. A bedroom, an office with the door shut, a parked car. Quiet helps but isn't required — sounds will become part of the practice, not enemies of it. You do not need a dedicated space, an altar, or a view of anything. You need a seat and a door that closes.

**On what.** A chair is fine — let this be said clearly, because the cross-legged-on-a-cushion image keeps more people from starting than any other single obstacle. Sit toward the front half of the chair if you can, feet flat on the floor, so your spine supports itself rather than slumping into the backrest; if your back needs the backrest, use it. (The full posture discussion is next lesson; for today: sit so you're stable and upright-ish, hands on thighs or in your lap, and stop optimizing.)

**Timer.** Set one — ten minutes — and put the phone face-down out of reach, or use a dedicated timer. The timer matters more than it seems: without it, a corner of your mind spends the whole sit checking whether it's been long enough, which is a distraction you can delete for free. With it, the duration question is *closed*, and you can give the sit away.

**Eyes.** Closed, or lowered to a soft unfocused gaze at the floor a few feet ahead. Closed is easier for most beginners; lowered is better if you're sleepy. Either is correct.

## The Instruction

The whole method fits in four sentences. Rest your attention on the physical sensation of breathing. When you notice your attention has wandered off, acknowledge where it went — lightly, without commentary — and return it to the breath. Repeat until the timer sounds. That's the entire exercise; everything below is footnotes to it.

**Finding the breath.** "Attend to the breath" means the *sensation*, not the idea. Pick one location and stay with it for the whole sit: the nostrils (coolness in, warmth out — subtle, good for focus), the chest (rising, falling), or the belly (the expansion and release — the easiest and most calming anchor for most beginners; start here if unsure). Don't rotate between locations mid-sit; a moving anchor is barely an anchor. And breathe *normally*. You are not doing a breathing exercise; you are watching a process that runs itself — though you'll discover, comically, that observed breath immediately turns self-conscious, like walking when someone's watching. That awkwardness fades within minutes. Let the breath be however it is: short, shallow, irregular — the instruction is to feel it, not to fix it.

**The first minutes.** Typically: a settling. The body downshifts a notch; the breath finds you. Then the mind, having tolerated novelty for ninety seconds, resumes broadcasting — and here is where your Unit 2 education pays off. You will "come to" mid-thought, realizing you've been gone: planning dinner, replaying a conversation, narrating this very meditation. That moment of realizing — the little "oh" — is the most important event in the entire sit. Do not spend it on self-assessment. The untrained reflex is a flash of *ugh, I'm bad at this*, followed by re-gripping the breath like a dropped phone; the trained move, and you can train it from sit one, is what the teachers call touch-and-go: note where you were ("planning" — one quiet word, if it helps), and step back to the breath the way you'd step back to the curb. No trial, no verdict, no run-up. The return is the rep, and a gentle return is a *cleaner* rep than a violent one, for reasons Unit 4 will wire into you: the practice is training your relationship to distraction as much as your attention, and every self-scolding return practices scolding.

**Counting, if you want scaffolding.** Many traditions offer the beginner a counting frame: count each exhale, one through ten, then start over; lose count (you will), start at one — cheerfully, since losing count is just the wander-signal made audible. Counting gives the attention a job with a built-in wandering detector. Use it for the first weeks if the bare breath feels too slippery; drop it when it becomes furniture.

**What else will happen.** An itch, guaranteed — treat it as optional curriculum: try watching it for five breaths before scratching (it often dissolves; if not, scratch mindfully and return — no heroics, per the fuller protocol in Unit 4). Sleepiness, especially if you sit after meals or in soft chairs — open the eyes, sit taller, take one deeper breath. Restlessness, the fizzing why-am-I-doing-this urgency around minute six — this is the practice's first real teaching, arousal chemistry you can now name (L7, L11), and it passes if unfed. Odd intervals of genuine quiet — pleasant; note them and return, without chasing a repeat, because chasing is how a good sit poisons the next ten (the "chasing calm" trap, Unit 4's beat). And emotion, sometimes, surfacing unannounced — for ordinary sadness or irritation, the instruction is the same curious attention as the breath gets; this course's full protocol for strong emotion is three lessons ahead, and the safety notes for practice when you're carrying heavy history are in Unit 5's adverse-effects lesson — a real topic this course treats with respect, not fine print.

**Ending.** When the timer sounds, don't eject. Take thirty seconds: notice the state you're in — whatever it is, including "annoyed and unchanged," which is a legitimate first-sit result — open the eyes if closed, and let the transition be part of the sit. The peak-end principle applies to practice too: how you leave the cushion colors what your memory files under "meditation," and your memory's file decides whether you return tomorrow.

## What Success Looks Like

Now the calibration that protects the habit. A "good" first sit, by the standards of everyone who has ever taught this: you sat for the whole timer, you noticed the mind wandering *at all*, and you returned it *at all*. That's it. Forty wanderings and forty returns is a strong session — forty reps (L1's arithmetic, now lived). What a first sit will *not* deliver, and was never going to: emptiness, bliss, transformation, or even reliable relaxation — the misconceptions demolished in Lesson 5, now standing between you and disappointment right where we installed them. The research timeline, honestly restated: subjective benefits (a touch more ease, quicker catches in daily life) commonly report within a couple of weeks of daily practice; the measurable attention and reactivity changes of Unit 2 arrive on the scale of weeks-to-months; and the dose-and-habit evidence — how little counts, why consistency beats duration — gets its own lesson at this unit's end.[^1] Today's sit is not supposed to produce results. It is supposed to produce *the second sit*.

So: ten minutes, today, before the reading momentum fades. Then come back, because the next lesson handles the thing you'll have noticed within ninety seconds of sitting down — the body you did it in: what actually matters about posture (less than you fear), what genuinely helps (more than you'd guess), and why every tradition fussed about it just enough and no more.
