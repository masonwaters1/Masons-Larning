Somewhere in your first sit, probably around minute four, you had a posture thought: *am I doing this right? Should my legs be crossed? My back hurts — is that allowed?* This lesson answers the body questions completely, and its governing principle can be stated at the top: posture matters just enough to get right, and not one degree more. The traditions fussed over sitting position for real reasons — some biomechanical, some attentional, all practical — but the fussing has been inflated, in the imported image of meditation, into a lotus-legged gatekeeping that excludes anyone with ordinary Western hips, a desk-job back, or a body that hurts. The clinical protocols cut through this decades ago: MBSR has always taught in chairs, and no outcome study has ever hinged on anyone's ankle placement.[^1] What follows is the honest anatomy of the sitting question: what posture actually does for practice, the menu of legitimate options, the few details worth caring about, and the permission slips most beginners need in writing.

## What Posture Is For

Strip the mystique and the seated posture solves an engineering problem with two failure modes you already know from Unit 2's vocabulary — the two ways attention degrades. Slump, soften, recline too far, and the arousal dial (your locus coeruleus, L11) drifts down: dullness, drowse, the sit dissolving into a nap with extra steps. Perch rigidly, brace, strain, and the dial pins high: tension, fidget, the white-knuckle sit. The classical instruction — upright *and* relaxed, alert *and* at ease — is precisely the middle of that dial, encoded as biomechanics: a spine stacked so it's carried by structure rather than muscle, which permits relaxation without collapse, wakefulness without effort. That's the whole secret. Every specific instruction below is in service of those two words, and any posture achieving them is correct — the tradition's own images say as much, since the historical postures of practice include sitting, standing, walking, and lying down, a fourfold list as old as the root texts. Posture is also, note, your first *interoception* assignment (L9): the instruction to feel the body's uprightness, the contact points, the balance, is body-scan training smuggled in before the body scan — which is why teachers treat settling into posture as part of the practice rather than preparation for it.

## The Menu

**The chair,** first and unapologetically, because it is where most modern practice happens and where this course suggests you stay unless drawn elsewhere. Sit toward the chair's front half, feet flat, hip-width, knees at roughly right angles (a cushion under the feet if the chair is tall; under the sit-bones if your hips sit low). Let the spine rise out of the pelvis — the cue that works for most people: imagine the crown of the head drawn gently upward by a thread, then let everything hang from that. Use the backrest if your back requires it; a small cushion at the lumbar curve keeps supported sitting from becoming slumped sitting. A chair sit meeting the upright-and-relaxed standard is not the beginner version of the real thing. It is the real thing.

**Floor sitting,** for those whose bodies allow and prefer it. The essential mechanic, missed by nearly everyone who tries and abandons it: *height*. Sitting cross-legged flat on the floor forces most adult hips to tuck the pelvis and round the spine — hence the aching back that convinces people floor-sitting isn't for them. The fix is elevation: a firm cushion (zafu) or folded blankets raising the sit-bones several inches, so the knees fall *below* the hips and the pelvis can tip slightly forward, stacking the spine. From there, the leg arrangements in ascending order of hip demand: simple cross-legged; Burmese (both shins on the floor, one in front of the other — the most sustainable for most Westerners); half-lotus and full lotus, which are hip-flexibility achievements, not spiritual ones, and buy you exactly nothing on the attention networks. **Kneeling** (seiza), astride a cushion or low bench, suits many backs that dislike cross-legged sitting — spine stacks easily, no hip flexibility required. **Standing** is a legitimate and underused option, especially for sleepiness or a protesting back: feet hip-width, knees soft, same crown-thread cue. And **lying down** — on the back, knees bent or legs extended — is the standard posture for the body scan (next lesson) and a valid choice for anyone whose pain, disability, or exhaustion makes sitting counterproductive, with one honest warning label: the horizontal arousal dial reads *sleep*, so lying practice fights drowsiness; eyes open, forearms raised at the elbows if you need a dead-man's-switch, and no shame whatsoever if you doze — but know the trade you're making.

## The Details Worth Having

**Hands:** on the thighs or stacked in the lap — wherever the shoulders can forget them. **Shoulders:** the highest-yield check in the whole posture; roll them up and let them drop once at the start, then again whenever you notice them creeping toward your ears (they will — shoulder-creep is a live stress readout, L7 made flesh). **Jaw and face:** unclench the jaw, let the tongue rest, soften the eyes and forehead — facial tension and mental tension travel together closely enough that releasing one measurably eases the other. **Chin:** slightly tucked, so the neck lengthens — a lifted chin strains; a dropped chin drowses. **Stillness:** aim for it *loosely*. Stillness supports concentration — every fidget is a mini-distraction with a motor component — but it is a training aid, not a vow: when genuine pain (as opposed to mere discomfort) arises, the instruction across both clinical protocols and sensible tradition is to move, *mindfully* — register the intention, adjust with attention, resettle — which converts the adjustment from a defeat into a rep. The distinction between workable discomfort and body-warning pain, and the practice of investigating the workable kind, is Unit 4's territory; the rule here is simpler and non-negotiable: sharp, numb, tingling, or joint pain means adjust, full stop. This course's position, against a certain machismo that haunts retreat culture: no attentional benefit justifies training your nervous system to override injury signals — that is anti-interoception, the opposite of the skill.

**Eyes, revisited:** closed turns the volume down on the world and up on the mind — better for busy environments, riskier for sleepiness and, for some trauma histories, for feeling unmoored (eyes-open is the standard trauma-sensitive modification, previewed here and detailed in Unit 5). Lowered soft gaze keeps an anchor in the room. Switch freely between sits; consistency within a sit.

## Permission Slips

In writing, because beginners need them. You may meditate in a chair forever. You may lean against the wall. You may lie down because your body hurts, and it still counts. You may shift position when you need to — mindful adjustment is practice, not failure. You may sit for ten minutes in work clothes on the edge of the bed; the practice does not check your outfit. Your inflexible hips are not a character flaw, your creaky knees are not an obstacle to awakening, and nothing about the attention circuitry of Unit 2 cares what your ankles are doing. The traditions' real posture teaching, under all the iconography, was two words long — upright, relaxed — and its purpose was always to make the body *forgettable*: stable enough, comfortable enough, that attention can leave logistics and go to work.

Which it now can — and its next assignment reverses the trick. Having arranged the body so attention could ignore it, the second formal practice of the curriculum does the opposite: it makes the body the entire object, sweeping attention slowly through it from the soles of the feet to the crown, region by region, waking the interoceptive channel of Lesson 9 on purpose. It is the practice MBSR teaches *first*, before even the breath — for reasons worth understanding — and it is the closest thing the curriculum has to a guided tour of the felt body. To the body scan.
