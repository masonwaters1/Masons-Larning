Run the arithmetic on your new practice. Twenty minutes of sitting a day — a solid commitment — is about two percent of your waking hours. If mindfulness lived only on the cushion, you would be training a skill for use in a gym you visit briefly and never leave equipped; and the wandering-mind statistic from Lesson 8 — nearly half of waking life spent elsewhere — would remain untouched in the other ninety-eight percent. The traditions never intended that split. In the root framework from Lesson 2, mindfulness was to be established in *all* postures — walking, standing, sitting, lying down — and in eating, dressing, speaking, and falling silent; the sit was the laboratory, and life was the field deployment. Modern teaching calls the field half **informal practice**: no timer, no posture, no added minutes — the activities you were going to do anyway, done with the attention you've been training, on purpose. This lesson is its methods, its flagship exercises, and the honest note about what informal practice can and cannot replace.

## The Principle: One Thing, Felt

Every informal practice is the same practice wearing different clothes, and the uniform underneath is this: *do the one thing you are doing, and feel it while it happens* — anchored in the senses, not the commentary. The structure is exactly the sit's: the activity is the anchor; the mind leaves (to the podcast you could be playing, the argument you could be rehearsing); the leaving gets noticed; attention returns to the sensation at hand. Catch-and-return, the rep you've drilled, now performed with wet hands or a fork. Two upgrades make it stick. First, *pick designated reps, not a lifestyle*: "be mindful all day" is a resolution with a half-life of hours; "the first sip of coffee, the shower's first minute, the walk to the car" are appointments attention can actually keep. The habit research is unambiguous that anchoring a new behavior to an existing routine — after I pour the coffee, I will taste the first sip with full attention — is how it survives past Thursday; next lesson builds on exactly this machinery.[^1] Second, *use transitions*: the seams of the day — doorways, red lights, the moment before unlocking the phone, the pause after ending a call — are natural bells. Some practitioners literalize this: one conscious breath at every threshold. Thich Nhat Hanh's community famously used the telephone's ring as a mindfulness bell — three rings enjoyed as three breaths before answering — and the modern translation writes itself: the notification you were about to lunge at is a bell, and one breath before checking it is a complete practice.

## The Flagship Exercises

**Eating.** The famous first exercise of every MBSR course is a single raisin, eaten over five minutes — examined, smelled, placed on the tongue, chewed with full attention — and its point is not the raisin: it is the demonstration, unforgettable once experienced, of how much sensation a routine act contains and how completely autopilot has been eating your meals for you. The daily version needs no ceremony: the first three bites of any meal, tasted — actually tasted, utensil down between them. The research bonus is real if modest: eating attentively slows pace and improves satiety detection (the stomach's fullness signal runs on a lag that speed outruns — interoception again, L9), and mindful-eating components appear in credible weight-management and disordered-eating protocols; the honest grading is "useful adjunct with decent trials," not a diet.[^1]

**Walking.** The formal version is a practice in its own right, taught on every retreat: a short path, ten or twenty paces, walked slowly — attention in the feet: lifting, moving, placing; the weight pouring from heel to toe; turning with attention at the end and returning. It sounds absurd and is, for restless minds and restless days, often *easier* than sitting — the body's motion gives attention a moving anchor and burns the fidget. The daily version drops the slow motion: any walk — parking lot, hallway, block — with attention in the soles and the swing, senses open, phone away. Movement practices deserve their footnote here: MBSR's mindful yoga, tai chi, and the runner's or swimmer's absorbed attention are all the same family — the body scan at speed.

**The dishes, and the chores.** The classic teaching example, from Thich Nhat Hanh: wash the dishes to wash the dishes — warm water, slippery ceramic, the arm's motion — rather than to *get them done*, which locates you in a future that keeps receding. Chores are ideal informal practice precisely because they're sensory-rich and cognitively empty: folding laundry, showering (the most reliably available five minutes of sensation in your day, currently spent in the conference room of your head), chopping vegetables. None of this makes chores mystical. It makes them *actual* — time you were alive for instead of fast-forwarded through — and it converts the day's dead zones into training volume at zero cost.

**Listening.** The deepest informal practice, and the one Killingsworth's data flagged: conversation was among the only activities where minds reliably stayed present. Practice it deliberately — attention on the person, their actual words and face, noticing the urge to compose your reply while they're still talking (the conversational equivalent of mind-wandering) and returning to listening. The people in your life will register this one before you do.

## The Honest Note: Field Versus Gym

Can informal practice *replace* the sit? The temptation is obvious — it costs no minutes — and the answer, honestly graded, is: partially, and not for everything. In its favor: the largest app-based dismantling trial to date (Lindsay and Creswell's work, met in Lesson 16) found that brief training emphasizing acceptance-in-daily-life produced real stress-reactivity benefits, and MBSR homework studies find informal-practice time correlating with some outcomes. Against the replacement fantasy: the correlational homework literature ties the *formal* minutes more consistently to the attention and symptom changes; the mechanisms this course has mapped — the sustained catch-and-return cycling of L6, the DMN retraining of L8 — plausibly need the sustained, undistracted reps that only protected time provides (you cannot interval-train in the checkout line); and every tradition that field-tested this question for centuries kept both halves. The stable conclusion: the sit builds the capacity; informal practice *deploys and generalizes* it — and each starves without the other. A practice that is all cushion produces the meditator serene at dawn and absent by lunch; a practice that is all raisins and doorways stays pleasant and shallow. Keep both books open.[^2]

Which leaves this unit one lesson short of complete, and it is the one that decides whether any of the previous six survive contact with your actual life. You now hold four formal practices and a field manual — and no answer yet to the questions that kill practices in week three: how many minutes actually count, what the research says about minimum effective dose, whether morning beats night, what to do about missed days, and how a practice becomes a habit rather than a resolution. To the dose, and the habit.
