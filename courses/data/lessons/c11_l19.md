Every skill this unit has taught now faces the only test that matters: week three. That's the historical burial ground of meditation practices — after the novelty, before the benefits — and what kills them there is rarely difficulty (you've handled the difficulty) but *dosing confusion*: how much is enough, when, how often, and what a missed day means. The wellness industry answers with vibes; this course closes its practicum by answering with the research — the honest dose-response evidence, the consistency findings, the timing questions — and then with the habit science that turns a decision into a default. Because here is the uncomfortable truth the field's own data teach: the modal outcome of learning meditation is quitting it. In the trials, sizable fractions of participants stop practicing when the program ends; among app downloads, the attrition curves are brutal. The skill of *continuing* is therefore not an afterthought to the curriculum. It is the last practice, and this is its lesson.[^1]

## What Counts as a Dose

**The honest minimum.** The research floor keeps dropping, and honestly reported it looks like this: trials using around ten to fifteen minutes a day — including well-controlled app studies — have found measurable effects on stress, mood, and attention within two to eight weeks; Zeidan's lab produced attention and pain findings from four twenty-minute sessions; and the Lindsay–Creswell smartphone trials found fourteen days of roughly fifteen minutes moved stress reactivity — *when* the training included the acceptance component (the attitude gear, load-bearing as ever). So the entry dose this course prescribes without embarrassment: **ten minutes a day**. Not because ten is magic, but because it clears the evidence floor for real-if-modest effects while sitting under the quitting threshold — and because the alternative prescription, MBSR's classic forty-five, is the right dose for an eight-week clinical program with a teacher and a cohort, and a demonstrated practice-killer as a cold-start solo demand.[^1]

**The honest ceiling, and the curve between.** More practice does more — the dose-response signal runs through this whole course, from homework-minutes correlations in the clinical literature to the retreat tier to L12's monks — but the curve is *gradual and unglamorous*: the meta-analytic association between home-practice time and outcomes in the eight-week protocols is real and small, and nothing in the data supports the idea that benefits switch on at some heroic threshold. Translation: twenty minutes likely beats ten by a bit; an hour is a practitioner's dose, not an entry fee; and the marginal minute matters less than the next principle, which the data support more strongly than any duration claim.

**Consistency beats duration.** Ten daily minutes outperforms seventy weekly ones, for reasons this course has already built: the skill is catch-and-return frequency across your *days* (the informal deployment of L18 rides on recency — a morning sit primes the day's catches), habit formation runs on repetition-in-context rather than volume (the automaticity research is unequivocal that frequency, not intensity, builds the default), and the psychology of the long gap is toxic — a week off converts "I meditate" into "I used to meditate," a re-entry cost no marathon Sunday session repays. The practitioner's rule of thumb, endorsed here: shrink the sit before you skip it. A three-minute sit on a chaotic day is not a token; it is the habit voting for itself, and the habit is the asset.

## Timing, Placement, and the Missed Day

**When.** The trials show no clear winner by clock time, so the evidence-based answer is: whenever you'll actually do it, with a structural argument for morning — willpower and schedule-integrity degrade across the day, and a practice placed before the day's chaos claims it can't be preempted by it. Specific slots with real constituencies: on waking (pro: unclaimed time; con: sleep inertia — sit up, eyes soft-open); with the coffee ritual (excellent anchor); lunchtime reset; the commute's end as a transition rite; evening (pro: decompresses; con: the drowse — and note the sleep findings are about mindfulness *programs* helping insomnia, not about bedtime sits, which for many become naps). Avoid only the food coma and the last minutes before sleep if alertness is your struggle. **Where:** same seat daily if possible — context is habit's strongest cue, and a chair that means "sit" will eventually start the practice for you. **The missed day:** the habit research's most practical finding, from Lally's automaticity study, deserves framing on your wall — missing a single day made *no detectable difference* to habit formation; what breaks habits is the second miss becoming a pattern, and what prevents that is a pre-decided rule. Adopt the athlete's: **never miss twice.** No streak worship (streaks convert one bad Tuesday into an identity crisis; the Lally data say the Tuesday was noise), no make-up doubles, no self-prosecution — the metta lesson applies to your practice record too. Miss, shrug, sit tomorrow.

## Building the Default

The assembly, from the behavior-change literature, in four moves. **Anchor it:** attach the sit to an existing daily event with an implementation intention — *after I pour the morning coffee, I sit for ten minutes* — the after-X-I-do-Y format whose meta-analytic record for converting intentions into behavior is among the strongest in psychology. **Shrink the start:** if ten minutes meets resistance in week one, begin with five, or three; the founding weeks are buying automaticity, not effects, and a dose you keep beats a dose you admire. **Reduce friction to zero:** timer app on the home screen (or the chair, the cushion, the corner pre-staged); every removed step is compliance purchased in advance. **Track lightly, and expect the trough:** a calendar check-mark exploits the completion circuitry without streak-fragility — and mark your expectations honestly for the arc ahead: early sessions carried by novelty; a weeks-two-through-four trough where novelty is gone, benefits are budding, and boredom auditions daily (this is where you were always going to quit; you are reading this lesson so that you don't); then, on the automaticity timeline — median around sixty-six days in the Lally data, ranging widely by person — the day you notice you sat without deciding to. That is the finish line of this unit: not an experience, but a *default*.

And mark what you now hold, because the practicum is complete. A first sit and its choreography (L13); a body arranged and forgiven (L14); the scan (L15), the sky (L16), the heart (L17); a field manual for the other ninety-eight percent (L18); and now the dosing and the scaffolding to keep all of it alive past week three.
