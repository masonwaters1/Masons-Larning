The last lesson ended with a promise: the single best picture the tradition possesses of how a man is eaten by what he refuses to see. Here it is, and it comes — with no apology — from a children's book.

Jack Kent's *There's No Such Thing as a Dragon*, published in 1975 and analyzed by Jordan Peterson in university lectures with a seriousness that reliably startles his students, runs twelve pages.[^1] Billy Bixbee wakes to find a dragon in his bedroom — cat-sized, friendly. He tells his mother. His mother delivers the sentence that drives the entire plot: *there's no such thing as a dragon.* So the dragon is not fed, not named, not patted — and it grows. By lunch it fills the hall. Soon the house rides down the street on its back, and Mr. Bixbee, coming home to an empty lot, must chase his own address. Only when Billy finally insists — "there IS a dragon. A very BIG dragon" — and pats it on the head does the creature begin to shrink, arriving back at cat size by the last page. Mother: "I don't mind dragons *this* size. Why did it have to get so big?" Billy, with the line Peterson calls one of the wisest in children's literature: "I'm not sure, but I think it just wanted to be noticed."

That is the whole mechanism, and this lesson's work is to unfold it at adult scale — because the man at the bottom did not lose his house to a monster. He lost it to a cat that nobody in the house was permitted to mention.

## The Anatomy of the Ignored Thing

Take the story apart and four gears appear, each one already half-familiar from the unit so far — the dragon is the road to ruin drawn for children.

**First: the dragon starts small.** This is Lesson 8 in a single image. Nothing arrives house-sized. The drinking arrives as unwinding; the distance in the marriage arrives as one tired week; the debt arrives as one bridge month on the card. The catastrophes of the man's file were, without exception, once cat-sized — approachable, pattable, solvable in an afternoon. The horror of the story is not the dragon. It is the arithmetic between page two and page eight.

**Second: the growth is fed by denial specifically.** Not by time — by *denial*. This is the story's precision, and it is where Kent's book outdoes most clinical writing: the dragon grows at exactly the rate it is unacknowledged, because — Billy's diagnosis — it wants to be *noticed*. Translate: problems signal. The overdraft notice, the wife's escalating bids for attention, the body's morning shake — each is a knock at a door, and an unanswered knock does not stop; it becomes pounding. The family systems literature has documented the domestic version for decades under the heading of the "elephant in the room": households organize themselves around the unmentionable — the drinking that everyone tracks and no one names — and the organizing itself, the vigilance and the silence and the routed-around conversations, becomes a second pathology on top of the first.[^2] The unnamed thing does not merely persist. It *recruits* — bending every family member's behavior around its shape, teaching children the house rule that seeing is dangerous. Many a man at the bottom learned dragon-blindness in exactly this school, decades before he had a dragon of his own; the adult children of alcoholics literature calls its first commandment *don't talk*.[^3]

**Third: the mother's sentence is the engine.** Note who denies the dragon. Not Billy — he sees it plainly, on page one. The denial is delivered by authority, as a statement about *reality itself*: there's no such thing. This is subtler and worse than avoidance. Lesson 10's man knew the envelope existed and dodged it; the Bixbee protocol removes the thing from the shared world entirely, so that facing it now requires not just courage but a kind of *insubordination* — contradicting the official reality of the household. Every marriage, every family, every man's inner household maintains such an official reality, and the tradition's psychologists are unanimous about what falls outside it: everything shameful, frightening, or inconvenient to the current arrangement. Jung's name for the exiled material was the shadow, and his formulation of the growth law is the one this course flagged in Unit 1 and now meets at full strength: the less the shadow is admitted to consciousness, "the blacker and denser it is" — and, in the sentence that could caption Kent's book, *when an inner situation is not made conscious, it happens outside, as fate.*[^4] The Bixbees do not face their dragon, so their dragon happens to them — as a house physically carried away. The man who would not face the drinking meets it as a DUI. The unfaced marriage-distance arrives as papers, served, a fate. Nothing was fated. Everything unfaced merely *arrived*.

**Fourth: acknowledgment shrinks it — and is nearly free.** The story's ending is its most clinically accurate stroke. Billy does not slay the dragon. There is no battle, no expertise, no montage. He says that it exists, and touches it — and the monster that carried a house returns to lap-size. The claim hiding in that ending is radical and, the previous lesson showed, empirically supported: a large fraction of the dragon's mass was never the problem itself but the *avoidance* — the accreted dread, the vigilance, the organizing-around. Exposure deflates what it touches; the feared number in the opened envelope is almost always smaller than the envelope had become. Not always — some dragons, faced late, really are house-sized now, and the course will never pretend the ninth-year conversation costs what the first-year one did. But even then, the acknowledged dragon *stops growing*, and a static problem, however large, can be worked. Only the denied one compounds.

## The Hoard Under the Dragon

Kent's book, being for children, omits the motif that the older dragon-stories insist on — and Peterson does not omit it, because it converts the whole picture from warning into instruction. In the myths, the dragon has a *hoard*. Gold under Smaug; the ring in the dragon's keeping; the treasure, always, in the lair — and the lair is precisely where no one wants to go. The mythological claim, which Peterson renders as a rule of practical psychology, is that *what you need most is to be found where you least want to look*.[^5] This is not decoration. It states a structural truth about avoidance that the man at the bottom must have in hand before the unit's final audit: the avoided places are avoided *because* they contain what is most significant — the conversation dodged for nine years was dodged precisely because the marriage's real questions lived inside it; the unopened envelope holds not just the number but the man's actual financial position, which is to say the starting coordinates of any climb; the unmade call to the son contains the relationship. The dragon's lair is the map's marked X. A man's list of what he cannot face is, read upside down, a list of where his life is kept.

Which is why the traditions pair the dragon with the *voluntary approach* — the thing this lesson's whole apparatus has been building toward, and which Unit 7 will install as discipline. The hero-pattern's instruction is not "have no dragons"; every house grows them. It is: go toward yours *on purpose, while it is small, on your own schedule* — because the confrontation is coming either way, and the only variables under a man's control are the dragon's size and who picks the date. Owen's daily mortification, the recovery movement's fearless moral inventory, the exposure therapist's graded hierarchy, Peterson's advice to ask what you're avoiding and do the avoided thing first: one instruction, four dialects.[^6] The Bixbee family got their house back the day insubordination against the official reality became speakable. Nine words from a child: there is a dragon, a very big dragon.

But the honest reader will have noticed the question this lesson has quietly begged. If the dragon merely wants to be noticed — if acknowledgment is nearly free and the hoard is real — why does a man keep declining? Not from ignorance; by year nine even he knows the dragon's name. Something else is paying him to keep the denial running — a fuel that makes the descending step feel not like avoidance but like *justice*, that converts his ruin into a grievance and his grievance into a residence. The tradition holds that no substance is more corrosive to a man's soul, and it braids from three strands: resentment, deceit, and arrogance. That rope is next.
