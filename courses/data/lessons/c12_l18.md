Twice now this course has borrowed a concept ahead of paying for it. Lesson 4 used the contamination script to explain how a self-image fossilizes; the last lesson found it confiscating goals. This lesson pays the debt in full, because the science of life stories — narrative identity research, the field Dan McAdams built — turns out to hold something this course cannot proceed without: the single best-documented psychological difference between men who stay at the bottom and men who climb out. Not circumstances. Not severity of the record. The *shape of the story* — and the evidence for that claim, which sounds like a novelist's conceit, is strong enough that Unit 7 will build the climb's first discipline directly on it.

## The Science of the Second Author

Start with what the field actually claims, because it is more precise than "we tell ourselves stories." McAdams's research program, running since the 1980s, holds that identity itself — the answer a person carries to *who am I and how did I get here* — takes the form of an internalized, evolving life story: a selective reconstruction of the past joined to an imagined future, with characters, turning points, and causal arcs.[^1] The method that made this testable is the Life Story Interview: hours of structured autobiography — high points, low points, turning points — transcribed and coded by blind raters for formal properties. And the properties turn out to be stable, scoreable, and predictive: how a life is *narrated* forecasts mental health and behavior years out, controlling for what actually happened in it. Two findings anchor the field, and both were built for this course.

**Story-shapes are real and few.** Across thousands of interviews, two sequence-forms recur so reliably they function as the field's primary code. In a *redemption sequence*, the narration moves bad-to-good: the suffering is not denied but is made to *yield* something — the divorce that broke me open, the cell where I finally read. In a *contamination sequence*, the movement is good-to-bad: the promotion that led to the drinking, the happy years revealed as the setup for the loss. Same events, opposite arcs — and the arcs are properties of the *telling*, demonstrably separable from the objective badness of the material, since raters find both shapes across every level of actual hardship.[^2]

**The shape predicts.** Contamination-heavy narrators show more depression, lower well-being, and lower generativity, cross-sectionally and prospectively; redemption-heavy narrators the reverse — and the crucial studies run the arrow forward: narrative coded at baseline predicts functioning *later*, and changes in narration during therapy precede symptom improvement rather than merely reflecting it.[^3] The story is not just the scoreboard. It behaves like part of the machinery.

## Maruna's Liverpool Discovery

Then a criminologist did the experiment this course would have designed. Shadd Maruna, studying ex-offenders in Liverpool, confronted the field's standing embarrassment: everyone knew *that* some persistent offenders desist, and the structural predictors — age, marriage, work — explained far less than they should. His Liverpool Desistance Study took a different instrument to the problem: McAdams's life-story interview, administered to two groups *matched on background* — same neighborhoods, same offense profiles, same bleak structural hands — one group persisting in crime, one group desisting. If circumstances were the story, the narratives should have matched. They did not, and the mismatch is one of the most important findings this course will cite.[^4]

The persisters told what Maruna called a *condemnation script* — and the reader of this course has already heard every line of it, because it is Units 1 and 2 in the first person: doomed from the start, product of the circumstances, no real choices, the self a passenger in a life driven by forces. It is the contamination sequence fused with Lesson 5's explanatory style, narrated as sober realism. The desisters — same streets, same records — told a structurally different story, the *redemption script*, with three load-bearing features. First: the "real me" was always there — the core self was never the offender; the crime years were a departure, an occupation by addiction or desperation, not an expression of essence. Second: an agentic turn, usually credited partly to *outside* help — a person, a program, a God — that believed in the real me before the evidence supported it. Third, and most striking: the wreckage repurposed — the past re-narrated as qualification, the ex-offender uniquely equipped to reach men still in it, the ruin converted to credentials for generative work.

Two things about this discovery must be pressed. The first is its scandal to common sense: the desisters' scripts were, by strict accounting, *less* accurate than the persisters' — the "real me was always there" claim is empirically dubious; the persisters' determinism cited genuine forces. Maruna faced this squarely and drew the conclusion that gives this lesson its edge: the stories that keep men out of prison are not the most factually defensible ones but the most *livable* — narratives selected, in effect, for what they make possible rather than for forensic accuracy. A man cannot act tomorrow inside a story in which he was never the author of anything; the condemnation script's very honesty about forces is what makes it a life sentence. The second is what the discovery does *not* say: it does not say desisters lied. The re-narration worked with real material — the kindness that had actually persisted through the offending years, the help actually received, the reach they actually had with younger men. The redemption script is an *edit*, exactly as Lesson 4 said the contamination script was — a different selection and arrangement of the same archive — and the finding is that archives support multiple true books, and the books have different sequels.

## The Course's Own Position

Now the course must locate itself carefully, because this material is easily misused in two directions, and both misuses are standing nearby.

The self-help misuse says: simply re-story yourself — speak the new narrative, manifest the redemption arc. The data will not carry that. Maruna's desisters did not talk their way out; the script *accompanied and enabled* years of concrete change — the new bonds, the work, the amends — and the therapy studies show narration and behavior moving together, each feeding the other. A redemption script asserted over an unchanged life is the cheap yes of Lesson 7, and the man at the bottom will smell it instantly, on himself most of all. The story is a necessary component, not a sufficient one — the operating system, not the whole machine.

The cynical misuse says: so redemption is just useful fiction — men who turn around are merely better self-deceivers. Here the course's deeper traditions step in with an alternative the psychology cannot rule out and Unit 4 will argue for directly: that the redemption script recurs because it is the *shape of something true* — that the desister who says the real me was always there, that help arrived from outside, that the wreckage is being repurposed for others, is groping toward the exact structure the theology asserts: an identity not exhausted by the record, a grace that precedes evidence, a providence that wastes nothing. McAdams himself documented that the redemptive self is, historically, a *religious* form — his American subjects were retelling, mostly unknowingly, a story their culture learned from Augustine and the conversion narrative and the spirituals.[^5] Whether the form survived because it is adaptive or is adaptive because it is true is precisely the question the course's title asks in narrative key, and it stays open, on the record.

What is not open is the practical finding, and the man at the bottom should leave this lesson holding it: his condemnation script — the one that feels like the hard-won truth about his life — is a *known form*, coded in studies, told in his exact words by the men who went back inside, and distinguishable, in matched samples, from the form told by the men who stayed out. His story has a shape, the shape has a track record, and the track record is bad. That is not yet hope. But it is the thing hope needs first: the discovery that the narrator's chair is a *position* — occupied, currently, by an editor with a documented bias — and not, as he believed, a window.

One scene, though, appears in both scripts — the desisters' and the persisters' alike — and the course must examine it before the unit descends further, because everything turns on which script it gets filed in. The bottom itself. Rock bottom: the phrase everyone uses, the moment recovery lore treats as the turn's precondition and the morgue data treats as a coin flip. What actually happens there is next.
