The course's title question — can a man change? — has been circling one distinction since Lesson 7 fixed it as the final exam: *new leaf or new man*. Behavior managed, or nature transformed. Every mechanism the psychology offers, honestly audited, operates on the first side of that line: habits re-trained, stories re-edited, explanations re-styled, environments re-engineered — modifications, sometimes profound ones, of an existing machine. The question of whether the machine itself can be *replaced* — whether there is any sense in which a man becomes ontologically other than he was — is a question no laboratory can even formulate. Reformed theology formulates it, and answers with the most radical claim in this entire course: regeneration — the new birth — a change the tradition describes not as the man's supreme achievement but as something *done to him*, beneath his faculties, at the root the faculties grow from. If the claim is true, it is the strongest possible answer to the course's question. This lesson lays it out with the care such a claim demands.

## The Doctrine in Its Own Words

The scriptural spine first, because the doctrine's language is stronger than any summary of it.

Ezekiel, to exiles who had wrecked everything: "I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you. And I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh."[^1] Note the grammar the tradition builds everything on: every verb is God's — *I will give, I will remove, I will put* — and the object is not the man's behavior but his *heart*, the biblical word for the control room Lesson 23 found corrupted throughout: the fountain of perception, desire, will. Not the stone heart polished, softened, or managed. Removed.

Jesus to Nicodemus — a moral success, not a wreck, which sharpens the point: "Unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God... That which is born of the flesh is flesh." Birth, as the tradition never tires of observing, is the one event in human experience to which the subject contributes nothing: no one assists at his own birth, schedules it, or earns it; it is done to him, and everything he ever does *follows from* it. And the wind image Jesus adds — blowing where it wishes, heard but not commanded — writes the doctrine's sovereignty and its mystery into the founding text together.[^2] Paul supplies the third register, and it is the bluntest: not sickness needing treatment but death needing resurrection — "you were dead in trespasses... God made us alive together with Christ" — and creation *ex nihilo*: "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation; the old has passed away."[^3] Sickness cooperates with its cure. The dead do not.

From these the tradition drew its systematic account, and its edges matter. Regeneration is *monergistic* — one worker, God alone; the man is passive in it as in his birth, which is precisely why it can reach the man of Lesson 17, whose agency read zero: the doctrine's rescue does not run on the faculty the descent destroyed. It is *sub-conscious* in the strict sense — worked beneath awareness at the root of the person, so that what the man experiences is never the operation itself but its effects: the first of which, the tradition says, is that faith and repentance become *possible* and then *actual* — not conditions the man meets to obtain the new heart, but the new heart's first beats.[^4] And it is *decisive*: an event, not a process — the process is sanctification, Lesson 29's subject, and confusing the two wrecks men in both directions.

## What Is Actually New

Here the doctrine must be protected from a misreading that would falsify every observable fact, and the tradition's own precision does the protecting. Regeneration does not claim the man's memories are erased, his record expunged, his habits dissolved, his brain rewired overnight, or his addictions chemically cancelled. The gallery ahead settles this empirically: Newton regenerate remained for years a slave-ship captain slowly waking; Bunyan regenerate fought despair for a decade; the new birth co-exists, in every documented life, with the old machinery still installed and running. So what, exactly, is new?

The tradition's most careful answer — Jonathan Edwards worked it out at book length while auditing a revival full of counterfeits — is: a new *disposition*, a new governing taste at the base of the soul; what Edwards called a new *sense of the heart*, as different from the old man's religion as tasting honey is from being told it is sweet.[^5] The regenerate man's faculties are the same faculties; what has changed is what they *relish* — the fundamental direction of love at the root. Augustine's categories, which Unit 7 will meet again: a man is what he loves, and the loves have been reordered at the source. Practically, in the testimonies the tradition has collected for centuries, the newness shows up exactly where this course's instruments would look: perception (the same Scripture, the same creation, the same neighbor suddenly *seen* — Lesson 14 showed perception is aim-dependent; here the deepest aim is replaced); appetite (sin still present, still tempting, but no longer *home* — the regenerate man sins against his new grain, and the difference between sinning with the grain and against it is, Owen will say, the whole difference); and warfare (the old machinery now *contested* — the very struggle the man reads as evidence of failure is, in the tradition's reading, the surest evidence of life, since corpses do not fight).

Which yields the doctrine's answer to the course's exam question, and it must be stated exactly: the new man is not the old man improved. He is a new principle of life implanted in the old man's premises — a seed in occupied ground, whose growth into the whole territory is the long war of sanctification. New leaf is behavior modified from the old root. New birth is a new root, from which new behavior grows organically, over years, with setbacks, against resistance — but grows, because the life is *in* it.

## The Honest Questions

Three questions press, and the course's standard of proof requires them faced.

*Is this distinguishable from a psychological event?* Sometimes not, from outside, in the short run — the tradition itself says so; Edwards wrote *Religious Affections* precisely because revival experiences, brain events, and new births look alike at the start, and his test is the course's own standard from Lesson 7: not the intensity of the experience but the *fruit across years* — the abiding reordered loves, the long obedience, the humility that grows rather than performs. Unit 5 will hold the conversion experiences to exactly this fire.

*Why does God regenerate some and not others?* Here the tradition answers with a doctrine — election — that this course will treat honestly in its place and will not pretend is comfortable. But note what the doctrine does for the man at the bottom *pastorally*, which is the reverse of its reputation: it locates the decisive cause of any man's turning outside every human ranking — outside merit, promise, likelihood, and record — which is why the tradition's evangelists preached to the least likely with a confidence no meritocracy could sustain, and why the roll call the next two lessons open is what it is: Manasseh, David, the thief on the cross. A grace that ran on likelihood would have skipped every one of them. Whatever else election means, it means *no file disqualifies*.

*And can a man do anything, if the work is God's alone?* The tradition's answer is its pastoral genius, and it lands on our man with precision: he cannot regenerate himself — Lesson 23 closed that door — but he can *put himself where the wind blows*. The means of grace, the old writers called them: the Word read and preached, prayer even from the belly ("out of the depths I cry" is a regeneration-adjacent psalm), the company of the praying, the asking itself — "how much more will your heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him."[^6] No man commands the wind. Any man can raise a sail. And the course notes, for its file, the strange convergence: this is exactly the geometry of Lesson 19's door — unschedulable, assistable — discovered by the psychology of turning points three centuries after the theology of regeneration mapped it.

The doctrine is now on the table: the strongest yes the course will hear, resting on claims its instruments cannot verify and its gallery keeps testifying to. What the doctrine sounds like *as a story* — the far country, the pigs, the coming to himself, the father running — is the next lesson, and the course has been saving it since Lesson 1: the Prodigal Son, read at walking pace, as the master text of everything this unit has said.
