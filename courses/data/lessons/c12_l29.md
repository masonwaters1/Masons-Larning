The unit has now made its two great claims — the verdict settled in an instant (justification), the nature replaced at the root (regeneration) — and an honest reader is entitled to the obvious objection: *then why does the turned man still struggle?* Why did David sin again after Psalm 51? Why do the gallery's converts fight their old appetites for decades — Newton captaining slave ships for years after the storm, Cash relapsing into the seventies? Why does the man at the bottom, if he turns, wake up the next morning with the same installed machinery Units 1–3 mapped — the same cues, the same braid offering its wages, the same grooves? If nothing changed, the doctrine is false. If everything changed, the mornings are inexplicable. The tradition's answer is a third doctrine, the one this lesson exists to install, because getting it wrong destroys recovering men in *both* directions: sanctification — the lifelong war that follows the instant verdict — change that is, in the tradition's precise formula, *definitive and progressive at once*: a decisive break, then a long campaign; already new, not yet finished.[^1]

## The Two Moments

The formula's first half the tradition calls definitive sanctification, and it must be stated first because the man at the bottom will otherwise hear "lifelong struggle" as "nothing really happened." Something happened. Paul's grammar throughout is *past tense about a death*: "our old self *was crucified* with him"; "you *have died*, and your life is hidden with Christ"; "sin will have no dominion over you" — not a resolution the man makes but a status conferred: the old regime's *dominion* — its legal claim, its throne — broken at the root in the regeneration of Lesson 25.[^2] The tradition's picture: the tyrant is deposed, decisively, once. What remains in the territory is not the king but the *insurgency* — the installed machinery, the trained appetites, the indwelling remainder Paul calls "sin that dwells in me" and Owen spent a book anatomizing: deposed, disarmed of final authority, and still shooting.

And so the second half: progressive sanctification — the actual campaign, fought in time, with means, against resistance, with real losses, toward a completion that arrives only at the end. Paul's own report from mid-campaign is the most comforting passage in the canon for the struggling, precisely because of who wrote it: "I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I keep on doing... wretched man that I am!" — the apostle, decades in, describing the exact civil war the man at the bottom will mistake for evidence of a failed conversion.[^3] The tradition's pastoral genius is its reading of that war: the *conflict itself is the evidence of life*. The unregenerate man of Unit 2 had no war — the descent was frictionless, the lies unresisted, the grain and the sin aligned. The new man sins *against* his grain, and hates it in the doing, and the hating — miserable as it feels — is the new nature registering. Owen's formula, which every recovering man should have on the wall: the difference is not the presence of sin but the *direction of the fight*.

## The Two Errors

Now the destroying errors, and the course names them carefully because the man at the bottom is a natural candidate for both, sometimes in the same week.

**The instant-perfection error.** Fed by testimony culture and by the honeymoon chemistry Unit 5 will document, the newly turned man concludes that the change, if real, should be *complete*: cravings gone, temper healed, the machinery uninstalled overnight. Then Tuesday comes — the cue fires, the braid whispers, perhaps he falls — and the syllogism executes: real change is total; mine was not total; therefore mine was not real; therefore *I* am not real, and the shame machine of Lesson 3 processes the relapse as identity-verdict, and the contamination editor files the whole conversion as one more good thing that turned bad. The relapse data of Lesson 7's standard shows where that filing leads: the most dangerous moment in a turned man's first years is not the first stumble but the *interpretation* of the first stumble. The doctrine of the long middle is the counter-interpretation, installed in advance: the war was forecast; the stumble is a battle, not the verdict; the verdict was rendered elsewhere, on other grounds, and does not fluctuate with Tuesday — get up, confess (1 John's provision is written *to the regenerate*: "if we confess... he is faithful and just to forgive"), and return to the campaign. The recovery movement rediscovered the same doctrine empirically and calls it the abstinence-violation effect: men who read a lapse as total defeat convert lapses into collapses; men taught in advance to read it as an event resume.[^4]

**The passivity error** is the mirror image, and subtler: if God does the changing — monergism, Lesson 25 — then the man's part is to wait, "let go and let God," and the struggle itself betrays fleshly self-effort. The tradition's answer is its most quoted paradox, and the man at the bottom should memorize the verse whole, both halves: "*Work out* your own salvation with fear and trembling, *for it is God who works in you*, both to will and to work for his good pleasure."[^5] Not fifty-fifty; both-a-hundred: God's working is not the alternative to the man's working but its *ground* — the energy in the effort, not the substitute for it. Regeneration was monergistic; sanctification is not: it is the one doctrine in the order of salvation where the man is summoned as combatant, commanded in the active voice on every page — put to death, put off, put on, flee, pursue, resist, run. Grace, the tradition says in a line Dallas Willard made famous and the Puritans lived, is opposed to *earning*, not to *effort*.

## The Means, and the Shape of the Campaign

Which returns the course to Owen, whose *Mortification of Sin* Unit 7 will deploy as the tactical manual, and whose strategic sentence belongs here as the campaign's motto: "Be killing sin, or it will be killing you" — *be killing*: continuous aspect, daily warfare, no armistice available, because Lesson 10's growth law never expires: the insurgency unfought is the insurgency compounding.[^6] The tradition's arsenal is unglamorous and tested — the means of grace of Lesson 25 now weaponized: the Word (the counter-map against the falsified one), prayer (the supply line), the sacraments and the congregation (Lesson 6's new mirrors, institutionalized), confession to another (Lesson 15's rule two), the sabbath, the disciplines — and the course flags, for Unit 8, the convergence it keeps finding: the habit science, the exposure protocols, the relapse-prevention plans are the means of grace's secular cousins, describing from below the same campaign the theology commands from above.

Two features of the campaign's shape, and the unit can close its doctrine.

*It is not linear.* The growth curve the tradition describes — and the biographies confirm — loops: seasons of advance, plateaus, ambushes at the point of triumph (the gallery is full of post-victory falls), and a strange deepening in which the maturing man feels *worse* about his sin as he becomes objectively better — sensitivity rising faster than sin declines, so that the saints report themselves the chief of sinners in perfect sincerity. The man at the bottom must be told this in advance or he will read the deepening as regression: increasing grief over smaller sins is the sign of a heart coming alive, not dying.

*And it ends.* The tradition calls the end glorification and stakes the whole campaign on its certainty: "he who began a good work in you *will bring it to completion*" — the outcome guaranteed by the same alien grounds as the verdict, which is what makes the long middle survivable: a war whose end is certain can absorb any number of lost battles.[^7] The man at the bottom has fought only wars he expected to lose, which — Lesson 5 showed — is why he stopped fighting. This doctrine hands him the one thing that reverses the shuttle box: a war he cannot ultimately lose, entered from a verdict he cannot forfeit, fought with an energy not finally his own.

The unit's system is now complete — diagnosis, verdict, new birth, the master story, two case files, and the long middle. One voice remains to be answered, and the course has heard him muttering since Lesson 24: the moralist, inside and outside the man, who insists this whole economy is too cheap — that grace this free must license the wreckage it forgives. The tradition's fiercest answer came from a man the Gestapo hanged, and it is next: cheap grace, costly grace, and why the freest gift in the world costs a man everything.
