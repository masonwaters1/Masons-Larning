The objection has been standing at the back of the room since Lesson 24, and it deserves its own lesson because it is not stupid, it is not new, and the man at the bottom will meet it in every voice that matters to him — the ex-wife's, the judge's, the elder brother's, and his own. It runs: *your whole economy is a moral hazard.* Declare the wreck righteous before he performs; guarantee the verdict against every future Tuesday; promise a war that cannot finally be lost — and you have removed every incentive that keeps men in line and handed the wrecker a doctrine-shaped excuse. Grace this free subsidizes the wreckage it forgives. The people he damaged, hearing him talk of pardon, will say it in plainer words: *how convenient.* The tradition's fiercest answer to this objection did not come from a tenured systematician. It came from Dietrich Bonhoeffer — a pastor who wrote it while training seminarians for an illegal church under the Third Reich, and who sealed the argument in the only currency objections of this kind respect: the Gestapo hanged him at Flossenbürg in April 1945, weeks before the end. His book opens with the distinction this lesson is built on: cheap grace and costly grace — and his first sentence goes straight at the wound: "Cheap grace is the deadly enemy of our Church."[^1]

## Cheap Grace, Anatomized

Bonhoeffer's definition deserves quoting nearly whole, because every clause names something the man at the bottom has seen or been: cheap grace is "the preaching of forgiveness without requiring repentance, baptism without church discipline, Communion without confession... grace without discipleship, grace without the cross, grace without Jesus Christ, living and incarnate." It is, he says in the formula that made the book famous, "the grace we bestow on ourselves" — grace as a *principle*, a doctrine assented to, an account of the world in which sin is forgiven-in-general, and therefore nothing in particular need change. The world's sin justified, he writes, rather than the sinner — the *situation* blessed, the man left as he was.

Notice with the course's instruments what cheap grace actually is, because the anatomy is familiar: it is the braid of Lesson 12 wearing vestments. Grace self-bestowed is deceit (the falsified map now theological: the file re-labeled *covered* without ever being opened); it is arrogance (the man seizing the Judge's prerogative and ruling in his own favor — the self-installed bench of Lesson 12, now issuing pardons instead of grievances); and it quietly feeds resentment (the cheap-grace man, challenged on his life, reaches instantly for *judge not* — the doctrine deployed as a weapon against every mirror). Cheap grace, in other words, is not an overdose of the unit's medicine. It is the *counterfeit* of it — manufactured by the same machinery Units 1–3 mapped, and distinguishable from the real thing by exactly the tests this course has already fixed. The man at the bottom has likely met it in himself: the season of talking forgiveness while changing nothing, the apology deployed as transaction, the God-talk that arrived suspiciously fast after the arrest. His people's cynicism about grace was trained by counterfeits. The objection at the back of the room is, in large part, the testimony of counterfeit's victims — and the tradition's answer begins by agreeing with them.

## Costly Grace

Then Bonhoeffer turns the coin: "Costly grace is the treasure hidden in the field; for the sake of it a man will gladly go and sell all that he has... It is costly because it calls us to follow, and it is grace because it calls us to follow *Jesus Christ*. It is costly because it costs a man his life, and it is grace because it gives a man the only true life." And the sentence that resolves the paradox the whole unit has been building: such grace is costly because it condemns sin, and grace because it justifies the sinner. "Above all, it is *costly* because it cost God the life of his Son... and what has cost God much cannot be cheap for us."

Unpack the resolution, because it answers the objection at its root rather than its surface. The objection assumed grace was cheap because it was free *to the receiver*. Bonhoeffer's answer: the price was not waived; it was *paid* — elsewhere, in full, by the giver. The course has met this shape twice and marked it both times: the father running through the village, taking the shame onto himself so the son walks in covered — the cost not cancelled, *transferred*; and Lesson 28's two rails, the guilt put away because it landed somewhere. The cross, in the tradition's account, is the transfer at cosmic scale: "he himself bore our sins in his body on the tree"; the free gift is free the way a rescued man's life is free — at maximal cost to the rescuer.[^2] And this re-prices everything downstream, which is Bonhoeffer's real argument: a man cannot receive at that price and live as if nothing happened, not because a rule forbids it but because the *arithmetic* forbids it — to treat the gift casually is to announce, publicly, an appraisal of what it cost, and the appraisal is the insult. Cheap grace is not too much forgiveness. It is too low a valuation — grace held cheap.

So the answer to *how convenient* is empirical, and the course has been assembling the data all along: watch what costly grace, actually received, does to men. Lesson 24 cited the law — forgiven much, loves much — and the gallery ahead will supply the exhibits: Newton, seized by a pardon he never got over, spending his last decades in the abolition fight and dictating, near death, the sentence that is this unit's summary: "my memory is nearly gone; but I remember two things: that I am a great sinner, and that Christ is a great Saviour."[^3] Zacchaeus, the parable's real-life twin: grace arrives at his table *first*, uninvited, before any restitution — and restitution erupts from him, fourfold, half his goods to the poor, not as the price of acceptance but as its *effect*.[^4] The pattern is the unit's ordering, verified in lives: performance-first produces compliance, concealment, and elder brothers; acceptance-first produces the only engine that ever rebuilt anything — gratitude with its sleeves rolled up. Grace does not license the wreckage. It is the only thing on record that has ever reliably *ended* it.

## The Cost, Itemized for the Man at the Bottom

But Bonhoeffer will not let the lesson end in the warm glow, and neither will this course, because "grace is free, and it will cost you everything" must be cashed out in particulars or it becomes a slogan — cheap grace about costly grace. What, concretely, will the man at the bottom pay? The units ahead will itemize; the invoice's headings belong here.

It will cost him *the case* — the grievance file of Lesson 12, his most productive asset, closed: a man under mercy has no standing to run a tribunal, and the Lord's Prayer welds his pardon to his pardoning in a clause he will choke on for years. It will cost him *the verdict* — strangely, the accepted verdict of Lesson 2, the identity of the destined failure, which was miserable and *his*, surrendered for an identity he did not author and cannot control. It will cost him *the anesthesia* — the treaty broken, the standards alive again, the whole war of Lesson 29 accepted in place of the ceasefire. It will cost him *the honest ledger* — Lesson 15 completed and spoken, then the amends of Unit 8, paid in real currency to real people, some of whom will not forgive him, which is part of the price. And it will cost him *the old company and the old sovereignty* — Bonhoeffer's summary of the call is a single terrible sentence, written by a man walking toward his own scaffold: "When Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die."[^5] Everything, in short, that Units 1–3 showed him living on. The far country runs a debtor's economy and calls it freedom; grace runs a gift economy and costs the recipient his entire previous self. That is the exchange rate, posted honestly.

The unit's work is done. The verdict question of Lesson 7 — whether for such a man there exists a judgment better than his own — has received the tradition's full answer: diagnosis to the bottom, verdict from outside, nature made new, exhibits on file, the long war declared, the counterfeit exposed, the price named. What the course has not yet examined is the *event* the gallery's men keep describing — the hour the exchange actually happened to them: the storm, the ward, the garden, the tent; the experience William James spent the most famous lectures in the psychology of religion trying to understand. The twice-born, and what actually happens in a conversion, is the next unit.
