The previous lesson closed on the monastic insight that the governed tongue has two disciplines, not one: the restraint that knows when to be silent, and the courage that knows when to speak. Both disciplines, notice, are personal — they concern the individual soul and its speech. But this unit's founding claim, that words make worlds, was never only about individuals; and the moment we ask what governed and ungoverned speech do to a *society*, we find that the deepest single statement of the answer was made not in Athens or Jerusalem but in the state of Wei, in the fifth century before Christ, by a wandering teacher out of office. Asked by a disciple what he would attend to first if a ruler gave him a government to run, Confucius gave an answer that has puzzled, irritated, and finally instructed readers for twenty-five centuries: the first necessity, he said, would be to rectify names — *zhengming*, to make names right. This lesson takes that answer with full seriousness, because it turns out to contain an entire political philosophy of language: the claim that social order is downstream of truthful naming, and that a civilization begins to unravel — quietly, procedurally, before any visible catastrophe — at the moment its words stop matching reality.

## The Cascade

The disciple, Zilu, reacted the way most first readers do: surely, master, this is wide of the mark — with a state to govern, *names* are your priority? Confucius's reply is one of the most tightly reasoned passages in the Analects, and its form matters as much as its content: it is a cascade, a chain of consequences, each link presented as following from the last. If names are not correct, he says, then speech does not accord with reality. If speech does not accord with reality, then affairs cannot be carried to success. If affairs cannot be carried out, then ritual and music do not flourish. If ritual and music do not flourish, then punishments miss their mark. And when punishments miss the mark, the people do not know where to put hand or foot. Therefore the noble person applies names that can be spoken, and speaks what can be carried into action; with respect to his speech, he leaves nothing to mere carelessness.

Walk the cascade slowly, because every link is a discovery. The first link claims that language is the *interface* between mind and world: if the names are wrong — if "surplus" means whatever the quota requires, if "temporary" means permanent, if "peace" means the suppression of the defeated — then no one can say true things even when trying, because the available words have been bent. The second link claims that cooperation runs on that interface: affairs — contracts, commands, plans, promises — are made of speech, and speech that cannot carry truth cannot carry coordination; a society that cannot describe its situation accurately cannot respond to it, any more than a pilot can fly on falsified instruments. The third link reaches into what Confucius calls ritual and music — his shorthand for the whole fabric of shared meaning: the ceremonies, courtesies, and common forms through which a community enacts its values and knows itself. These, he insists, live on truthful naming too: a ritual whose words have gone hollow becomes theater, and everyone can feel it. The fourth link is the jurist's: justice is the application of names to deeds — *this* act was theft, *that* one was not — and when the names have been corrupted, punishment detaches from desert and becomes arbitrary power wearing law's costume. And the final link is the one that should raise the hairs on a reader of this course: when all of this has happened, *the people do not know where to put hand or foot*. That is not a metaphor for inconvenience. It is a precise phenomenology of life under semantic breakdown — the paralysis of persons who can no longer tell what anything is called, and therefore what anything is, and therefore what is safe to do; a condition the twentieth century would manufacture at scale, and which this unit will meet again, in its full totalitarian form, in the lesson after next.

## What Rectification Means

It is tempting, and wrong, to read *zhengming* as mere dictionary hygiene — as if Confucius wanted better definitions. His own best illustration shows the deeper claim. Asked by Duke Jing of Qi about government, he answered in eight Chinese characters: let the ruler be a ruler, the minister a minister, the father a father, the son a son. The rectification of names, in other words, cuts in both directions at once. Sometimes the word must be corrected to match reality — call the famine a famine, call the defeat a defeat. But sometimes the *reality* must be corrected to match the word: a ruler who plunders his people carries the name of ruler without its substance, and rectification means either that he becomes what the name promises or that everyone stops pretending. Names, on this view, are not labels pasted onto things; they are compressed standards — each one carries a normative content, a description of what the thing at its best *is for* — and a society's names are its ideals in portable form. This is why Confucius, whom no one ever accused of underestimating ceremony, put naming *before* everything else in government: get the names right and you have gotten the standards right, and the standards, daily spoken, slowly pull conduct toward themselves. Corrupt the names and you have corrupted the standards in the very medium through which they are transmitted, and no amount of law downstream can compensate, because law itself is made of the corrupted material.

The reader of this course will hear the resonance with the unit's opening chords, and it is worth sounding explicitly. The Hebrew tradition this unit began from gives Adam, as his first recorded act, the naming of the animals — the human vocation presented as the truthful articulation of a world handed over in trust; and it presents God's own creative speech as the calling of things into their natures. The Greek tradition, which the next lesson takes up, would fight its own version of the battle in Plato's *Cratylus*, over whether names are mere convention or track something real. Confucius stands with the realists, but with a characteristic practical turn: whatever the metaphysics, he saw that a community's stock of names is a *commons* — an inherited, shared infrastructure of meaning, maintained or degraded by every speaker — and that like any commons it can be polluted faster than it can be cleaned. His conservatism about language, so often mocked as fussiness, is precisely the conservatism of the well-keeper who knows the whole village drinks downstream.

## The Modern Corruptions

Now bring the cascade forward, because our own commons is not clean. The gentlest corruption is *euphemism* — the name chosen to prevent the reality from being felt. Some euphemism is mercy, the courtesy that softens what needs no sharpening; but there is a class of euphemism whose entire function is to make the unacceptable sayable, and every reader can supply the specimens: the bombing that becomes air support, the killing of civilians that becomes collateral damage, the firing of ten thousand people that becomes right-sizing, the torture that becomes enhanced interrogation. George Orwell — whose full treatment waits two lessons ahead — called this the defense of the indefensible, language deployed not to express thought but to prevent it; and his analysis is Confucius's fourth link in modern dress: once the deed cannot be named, it cannot be judged, and punishment (or shame, its civilian form) misses the mark by design. A second corruption is *inflation*: the debasement of strong words through promiscuous spending, so that "genius," "trauma," "violence," "hate," "unprecedented" buy less each year — and when the real thing arrives, the treasury of language has nothing left of full value to spend on it. A third, subtler and closer to Confucius's own concern, is the corruption of *role*-names: teacher, journalist, judge, pastor, scientist, statesman. Each of these names, like "ruler," carries a standard — a promise about what the bearer is for — and each can be hollowed by holders who keep the title while abandoning the function, until the public, not fooled but unable to say exactly what has happened, simply withdraws its trust from the name itself. Much of what is called a crisis of institutions is, seen through this lesson, a crisis of unrectified names: the words still stand on the buildings; the people no longer believe the words; and they do not know where to put hand or foot.

The honest tension must be stated here, and it is serious: the power to "rectify names" is itself a power, and history's tyrannies have loved it. Regimes rename things constantly — enemies of the people, protective custody, reeducation — and enforce the new names with all the machinery Confucius's fourth link describes; the demand that everyone speak "correctly" has been, in living memory and in present tense, an instrument of exactly the semantic terror it claims to prevent. So the doctrine needs its criterion, and the criterion is the one this whole unit has been building: the direction of fit. Rectification in Confucius's sense bends *names toward reality* — or bends conduct toward the honest standard the name has always carried; corruption bends *reality's description toward power's convenience*, and punishes those who notice. The test is not who is doing the renaming but which master the renaming serves: the world as it is, or the story someone requires. A citizen equipped with that single question — *is this name trying to show me the thing, or hide it?* — possesses most of what this lesson has to give. And the personal application closes the circle with this course's namesake: Peterson's rule — be precise in your speech — is the rectification of names conducted at the scale of one life. His counsel that vague misery becomes manageable only when articulated, that the monster shrinks when named exactly, that you must specify what is wrong, what you want, and what you mean, is Confucian statecraft turned inward: the self, too, is a small polity, with its own euphemisms, its own inflated words, its own roles held in name only; and it too unravels when its names go wrong, one careless self-description at a time.

Yet naming, even rectified, is not the whole of speech's public power. Names set the terms; but cities are not moved by terminology — they are moved by *persuasion*: by the orator who takes the shared names and sets them on fire, for good or ill. The Greeks, who invented the free assembly, were the first to face in full what that power meant — and the first to be properly terrified of it. To rhetoric.
