The previous lesson ended on a distinction: names set a society's terms, but persuasion moves it. This lesson takes up persuasion itself — rhetoric, the deliberate art of moving souls with words — through the two greatest minds ever to disagree about it. The disagreement is not a museum piece. Plato's case against rhetoric and Aristotle's case for it constitute, between them, the permanent argument about the most consequential power a speaking species possesses: the power by which one voice can turn an assembly, launch a war, free a people, or destroy one. Everything this unit has established converges here. If words build worlds, then the person skilled at words holds a builder's power over other people's worlds; and the question the Greeks were the first to face in institutional form — because they were the first to make speech before a mass audience the actual mechanism of government — is whether that power can be made safe, and by what. Their answer, held in the tension between the two philosophers, will carry us directly into the darkness of the next lesson, where the twentieth century runs the experiment of speech-power fully divorced from truth.

## Why the Greeks First

A word on why this problem erupted where it did. In the Athenian democracy, and in the law courts of Greek Sicily where the first paid teachers of rhetoric appeared, speech was not commentary on power; it *was* power. Every citizen might have to defend his property or his life before a jury of hundreds with no professional lawyers; every question of war, taxes, and treaties was decided by whoever could carry the assembly. In such a world, the ability to speak persuasively was what martial skill had been to earlier ages — the survival art — and a profession arose to teach it: the sophists, itinerant masters who, for a fee, would train a man to argue either side of any case. Their very existence forced the question that had never needed asking before: what is this skill? Is persuasion a *knowledge* of something, or a mere knack? Does the man who can make the worse case appear the better possess wisdom, or a weapon? And the biographical fact that shadows the whole debate: the city that perfected public persuasion was the city whose assembly, persuaded, voted the Sicilian catastrophe, voted atrocities it repented the next morning, and put Socrates to death — with speeches.

## Plato's Prosecution

Plato watched that death, and his dialogue *Gorgias* is the prosecution of rhetoric written in its shadow. Socrates there interrogates the celebrated orator Gorgias and his hungrier disciples, and drives the art into a corner with a distinction that has never lost its edge. Genuine arts, Socrates argues, aim at the *good* of their object and can give an account of it: medicine knows the body's health and why its prescriptions serve it. But there exists a counterfeit shadowing every real art — a knack of producing not the good but the *pleasant*, guided not by knowledge but by memory of what gratifies. Cookery is that counterfeit to medicine: it flatters the palate while knowing nothing of health. And rhetoric, Socrates proposes with insulting precision, is cookery for the soul — a knack of flattery that produces *belief without knowledge*, gratifying an audience's appetites and prejudices while understanding nothing of the justice it constantly invokes. The orator, on this account, has no power worth the name, because he cannot deliver what a soul actually needs, only what it wants to hear; and the assembly he sways is a great beast whose keeper has learned its moods, not its good. The dialogue then lets the mask fall: young Callicles, tired of politeness, states what rhetoric's mastery is *for* — the strong doing as they please, convention be damned — and in Callicles, Plato shows us the terminus he feared: persuasive skill, unmoored from truth, arrives at the will to power, and the demagogue and the tyrant are its natural children. Reading the *Gorgias* after the twentieth century — after loudspeakers, after the Sportpalast — one does not find Plato paranoid.

But Plato did not leave the matter there, and the second word matters as much as the first. In the *Phaedrus* — a stranger, warmer dialogue, set outside the walls under a plane tree — he grants the possibility he had foreclosed: a *true* rhetoric, a legitimate art of moving souls. Its price, however, is everything the sophists sold their skill to avoid. The genuine rhetorician, Socrates argues there, must first actually *know the truth* about his subject — persuasion may deal in likelihoods, but only the man who knows the real can even manage plausible likenesses of it responsibly. And second, he must know *souls*: since speech's business is the leading of souls — the dialogue's word is *psychagōgia*, soul-leading — the true art requires a psychology, a knowledge of the kinds of souls and the kinds of speech that reach each kind, matching word to listener as the physician matches remedy to constitution. Rhetoric redeemed, in other words, is philosophy plus love: the knowledgeable soul, caring for another soul, choosing words fitted to lead it toward what is actually good. Plato's two dialogues thus set the boundary stones between which the entire subsequent history runs: rhetoric as flattery of the crowd, and rhetoric as medicine for it — the same skill, distinguished by nothing except its relation to truth and to the hearer's good.

## Aristotle's Defense

Aristotle, Plato's greatest student, accepted the boundary stones and then did the un-Platonic thing: he wrote the manual. His *Rhetoric* — the most influential book on persuasion ever composed, still the skeleton of every course on the subject — begins from a definition that quietly settles the moral question by relocating it. Rhetoric, he says, is the faculty of observing, in any given case, the available means of persuasion. A *faculty*, note: like strength, like intelligence — a capacity, in itself neither just nor unjust, whose moral color comes from its use; and if it is objected that the skill can do great harm, Aristotle answers that this is true of every good thing except virtue itself, and most true of the most useful things. His positive case for cultivating it rests on a premise of bracing realism about audiences: things that are true, he observes, have a natural tendency to prevail over their opposites — but only when the case is well made; before actual human beings, with their limited attention, their passions, and their inability to follow long demonstrations, truth *badly argued loses*. Therefore the person who cares about truth and justice is not permitted the luxury of despising rhetoric; if the knowledgeable refuse to persuade, the field is abandoned to the Callicleses, and it will be a strange comfort, watching the city burn, that one's own hands never touched the inflammatory instrument. Self-defense alone, Aristotle adds, would justify the art: it is disgraceful to be unable to defend yourself with your limbs, and speech is more distinctively human than limbs.

Then the anatomy, which every reader of this course should carry. Persuasion, Aristotle finds, travels on three roads at once. *Logos*: the argument itself — proofs and the rhetorical form of deduction he named the enthymeme, the syllogism with a premise left for the audience to supply, which persuades precisely because the hearer completes it and half-convinces himself. *Pathos*: the emotions of the audience — and here Aristotle, far from licensing manipulation, writes the first systematic psychology of emotion in the Western canon, book-length analyses of anger, fear, pity, shame, and their conditions, on the Platonic principle that leading souls requires knowing them; judgment, he observes, is not the same when we are grieved as when we are glad, so the honest speaker must attend to the hearer's state or be defeated by it. And *ethos*: the perceived character of the speaker — intelligence, virtue, goodwill — which Aristotle rates, in a remark that five decades of modern persuasion research has done nothing but confirm, as very nearly the most authoritative means of persuasion there is. We believe good men more fully and more readily; the speaker's character is, so to speak, the argument behind the arguments. The triad is not a bag of tricks. It is a description of how embodied, social, feeling creatures actually change their minds — and its ethical edge is that it holds for truth-tellers and liars alike, which is exactly why the truth-tellers must learn it.

## Souls and Cities, Moved

History has staged the debate ever since, and the great cases cut both ways with terrible symmetry. Demosthenes rousing Athens against Philip; Cicero holding the Republic together with his voice a little longer than its institutions deserved; Lincoln at Gettysburg, re-founding a nation's meaning in two hundred and seventy-two words; Churchill in 1940, of whom it was said that he mobilized the English language and sent it into battle; King at the Lincoln Memorial, taking the shared names of the American creed — the promissory note, the dream — and setting them alight so that a nation saw its own principles as if for the first time. Every one of these is Plato's *Phaedrus* vindicated: knowledge of the true, love of the hearer, words fitted to souls, cities moved toward their own good. And against them, the same century that gave us Churchill and King gave the counter-case in full: the Nuremberg rallies, the radio voice engineering hatred by technique — the big lie, the rhythmic crowd, the enemy named and named and named — Callicles with a microphone, and sixty million dead downstream of speeches. It will not do to say the dark orators were not *really* skilled; they were superbly skilled, and that is the entire problem. The skill is real, the skill is neutral, and the skill is loose in the world. Which is why this course's own subject belongs in this lesson's frame before it closes: whatever one's verdict on Jordan Peterson, the phenomenon this course studies is at bottom rhetorical — a teacher whose spoken word, carried by new technologies the Greeks never imagined, has moved millions of souls, and who has staked his career on the claim that articulate speech is the alternative to violence: that societies negotiate their futures with words, or eventually with weapons. That claim is Aristotle's wager restated. It is also, as Peterson's fiercest critics insist and as this course's later units will weigh honestly, a power that must be audited by the very standards this lesson has assembled — truth known, hearers' good served — because the standards, not the eloquence, are what distinguish the physician of souls from the cook.

The honest tension, then, stands as the lesson's conclusion, and it should be felt as tension rather than resolved too cheaply. Plato was right: rhetoric without truth is flattery ripening into tyranny, and the twentieth century is his footnote. Aristotle was right: truth without rhetoric is defenseless, and unilateral disarmament by the honest is not virtue but desertion. The only stable position is the narrow one both men, from their different sides, finally point to — eloquence as the *servant* of truth and of the hearer's good, suspect the moment it serves anything else. What neither philosopher fully imagined, though Plato came closest, was a regime that would seize the entire apparatus — the names, the arguments, the emotions, the speaker's manufactured ethos, and every channel of the word at once — and weld it into a machine for abolishing the distinction between true and false altogether. The Greeks feared the dishonest orator. The modern age built the dishonest *language*. To Orwell, Klemperer, and the corruption of the word itself.
