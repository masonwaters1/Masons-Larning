Unit 2 tried the theory and found it false. This unit tries something graver, and the change of register must be announced before the evidence is called: we move from the seminar to the morgue — from what Marxism claims to what Marxism, holding state power, did. The unit's method is fixed in this first lesson and enforced through the twelfth: names, dates, numbers, and wherever possible the perpetrators' own paperwork — the decrees, telegrams, and instructions that the regimes themselves wrote, preserved, and, after 1991, could no longer keep sealed. The course adopts this documentary discipline for two reasons. First, because the subject attracts inflation and deflation alike, and a case built on the archives survives both the propagandist who doubles the numbers and the apologist who halves them. Second, because the unit's thesis — stated now, argued across thirteen lessons — is not that Marxist regimes *contained* atrocities, as every large polity's history does, but that atrocity was *instrumental*: adopted as policy, theorized as necessity, administered as routine. And for that thesis the decisive exhibit is not the worst year but the first one. So the unit begins where the power began: Petrograd, the winter of 1917–18, and the proposition this lesson will document from the record — that the terror did not grow out of the civil war; the terror was there first.

## Week Six

The chronology is the spine of the argument, so fix the dates. The Bolsheviks took power on October 25, 1917 (old style). On December 7 — week six, before any organized White army existed, before a shot of the civil war proper had been fired — the Council of People's Commissars created the All-Russian Extraordinary Commission for Combating Counter-Revolution and Sabotage: the Cheka, under Felix Dzerzhinsky.[^1] Its founding mandate was not investigation but combat; its jurisdiction was defined by the elastic categories that would organize everything after — "counter-revolution," "sabotage," "speculation" — categories describing not acts but enemies. The other institutions of the first weeks complete the picture: the press decree closing opposition newspapers came within two days of the seizure; the Constituent Assembly — the freely elected body Lenin had demanded for a decade — met once in January 1918 and was dispersed by armed sailors when the Bolsheviks lost the election; the revolutionary tribunals were instructed to judge by "revolutionary conscience" rather than law. None of this was improvised under invasion. The serious civil war begins in the summer of 1918, with the Czechoslovak Legion's revolt in May as its usual marker. The machinery of extra-legal coercion predates it by half a year. The sequence matters because the standard defense — terror as tragic wartime necessity — requires the war to come first. It did not.

## The Doctrine

The second exhibit is that the terror was not concealed as an embarrassment but articulated as a theory, and by the principals themselves. Lenin's writings define the dictatorship of the proletariat with a jurist's candor as power resting directly on force and bound by no law — a definition, note, that concedes in one clause what a century of apologetics has labored to deny. His instructions during 1918 carry the doctrine into administration: the notorious telegram to the Penza Bolsheviks that August orders the public hanging of at least a hundred "kulaks" — explicitly *so that the population would see and tremble* — and demands the taking of hostages; other directives of the same season order "merciless mass terror" against ambiguous districts.[^1] Hostage-taking — the seizure and execution of persons for the acts of others, the categorical negation of individual guilt — was announced policy, published in the Cheka's own bulletins. And the class logic was made official in the instruction associated with the Chekist M. Y. Latsis, printed in the organ *Red Terror* in autumn 1918: investigators were not to seek evidence of deeds, but to establish the suspect's class origin, education, and profession — these, the instruction said, decide the fate of the accused. The formula deserves the emphasis this course gives it, because it is Unit 2's diagnosis made flesh: once the unit of moral analysis is the class rather than the person (L8), guilt detaches from action and attaches to category, and the police function follows the philosophy. When the assassinations of August 30, 1918 — Uritsky killed, Lenin wounded — brought the September 5 decree formally proclaiming the Red Terror, the decree did not create the practice. It ratified it, and supplied the occasion for its advertisement: mass shootings of hostages were announced in the hundreds in Petrograd alone, published as deterrent, which is to say as policy working as designed.

## The Scale and the Comparison

Numbers next, with the unit's standing care: ranges, sources, and the reminder that the full methodological accounting is L30's assignment. The Cheka's own fragmentary figures acknowledge some 12,000–13,000 executions through 1920 — a floor no serious scholar accepts as the total, since it omits summary killings in the provinces, the suppression columns, and the decossackization campaign of 1919, in which a Party directive ordered "mass terror against rich Cossacks, exterminating them to a man" and the Don paid with deaths credibly counted in the tens of thousands. Scholarly estimates for Cheka and tribunal executions across 1918–1922 run from around 50,000 at the conservative end to 140,000–200,000 and beyond; Nicolas Werth's archivally grounded reckoning in the collection this unit will meet repeatedly puts the plausible band in the low-to-mid six figures once the punitive expeditions are included.[^1] The course does not need the top of the range. Take the floor and run the comparison that fixes the phenomenon's novelty: the Tsarist autocracy — the "prison of peoples," policed by the Okhrana that Bolshevik memoir made a byword for despotism — carried out, by the standard scholarly counts, somewhere over six thousand death sentences in the *ninety-two years* from the Decembrists to 1917, the majority in the panic after 1905. The Cheka exceeded the autocracy's century in its first two years, likely in its first several months. This is the unit's recurring instrument — the before-and-after ledger — deployed for the first time: whatever the old regime's crimes, the revolution that came to abolish oppression multiplied its lethality by orders of magnitude, immediately, and by design.

## The Honest Counterpoint

Three defenses deserve their full weight. First: *the civil war was real, and all sides were savage.* True. The White armies' record includes mass shootings, the flogging of villages, and above all the pogroms — the anti-Jewish massacres of 1919, principally in Ukraine, which killed tens of thousands and stand among the era's worst atrocities; this course states that plainly and will not launder any side of a filthy war. Second: *the Bolsheviks inherited a brutalized country* — three years of world war, a collapsing state, arms everywhere; some level of violence was overdetermined. Also true. Third: *foreign intervention and the blockade* sharpened the siege mentality in which the terror grew. Granted again. But now the audit, because the counterpoint, fully granted, does not reach the thesis. The chronology stands: the Cheka, the hostage decrees, and the category-guilt doctrine precede the serious war — the necessity defense explains an escalation, not an origin. The asymmetry of *character* stands: White terror was atrocious and largely unsystematic, the crime of armies and warlords, denied or excused by its own side's spokesmen; Red terror was doctrinal — theorized in print by the regime's leaders (Trotsky wrote a book in 1920 defending it against Kautsky's socialist horror), announced in its press, administered by a permanent institution with a budget. Only one side's terror was a *philosophy*. And the test of the necessity defense is what happens when the necessity ends: the Whites were finished by late 1920, the interventions withdrawn — and 1921 brought not the dismantling of the Cheka but its busiest seasons (the next lesson's subject), followed in 1922 by its reorganization into the GPU: larger, permanent, constitutional. An instrument created for an emergency that outlives every emergency was never an emergency instrument. It was, as this lesson's title states and its documents show, policy.

One more finding, and the unit's arc depends on it. Everything documented here — the category enemy, the hostage logic, the police above law, the pedagogy of public killing — is the *complete toolkit* of what follows. Stalin's collectivization, the Terror of 1937, the Gulag: the coming lessons add scale, not kind. The claim that a humane Leninism was betrayed by a Georgian aberration must survive this lesson's documents, and the course submits that it cannot. But before the heir arrives, the founder's system must meet its first economic reckoning — because in these same years the doctrine was applied not only to enemies but to grain, and the result was the catastrophe that forced even Lenin to retreat. To War Communism, Kronstadt, and the first famine.
