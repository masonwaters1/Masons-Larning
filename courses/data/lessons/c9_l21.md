The NEP truce lasted seven years, and the course marks what the truce was: a standing refutation tolerated for tactical reasons. Under the mixed economy the doctrine despised, the country recovered to prewar output, the peasants fed the cities through the price system, and the Party held the "commanding heights" while privately regarding the arrangement — a socialist state balanced atop a hundred million smallholders — as a contradiction awaiting resolution. Stalin resolved it. Beginning in the winter of 1929–30, the regime undertook what he called the Great Break: the forced collectivization of Soviet agriculture, the liquidation of the "kulaks as a class," and the extraction from the collectivized countryside of the grain that would finance crash industrialization. The previous two lessons matter here as inventory, because nothing in the method was new — the category enemy, the quota, the hostage logic, the requisition, the sealed border — only the scale. What was new was the result: a peasant society of centuries broken in five years, and, in 1932–33, a famine that killed millions in the Soviet Union's best farmland while the state exported grain and posted guards at the borders of the starving regions. This lesson documents the campaign, gives the numbers with the course's usual ranges, and then walks into the genocide debate honestly — because on this subject the honest version is damning enough, and the course will not launder it in either direction.

## Dekulakization

The campaign opened with the removal of the village's natural resistance. The Politburo decree of January 30, 1930 — a document, like most in this lesson, unavailable to scholarship until the archives opened — divided the "kulaks" into three categories with numerical quotas attached: the first to be shot or sent to camps, the second deported to remote "special settlements," the third expropriated and resettled locally. The definitional problem was solved as L19's doctrine solves it: "kulak" was whoever the quota required — the owner of two cows, the employer of a seasonal hand, the vocal objector reclassified as a "kulak henchman." OGPU records count roughly 1.8 million people deported in 1930–31 alone, shipped in winter freight cars to the Urals, Siberia, and Kazakhstan, frequently to bare taiga where the settlement consisted of the order to build one; the special-settlement registers themselves record hundreds of thousands of deaths in the first years, children disproportionately among them.[^1] The course pauses on the mechanism rather than the horror: this was not mob violence but *administration* — quotas issued, fulfilled, over-fulfilled by ambitious provinces, tabulated in reports that survive. The remaining peasantry, reading the lesson as intended, entered the collective farms; the other reading of the lesson was the slaughter of livestock on a scale without historical parallel — roughly half the country's horses and cattle and two-thirds of its sheep were eaten or destroyed within four years rather than surrendered — an incentive verdict (Unit 2, again) written in carcasses, and a subtraction of draft power that agriculture would feel at the worst moment.

## The Terror-Famine

The collective farm was requisition made permanent: the state now set procurement quotas on grain it deemed its own, and 1930's good harvest was extracted so thoroughly that the Party mistook seizure for success. Then yields fell — weather, the dead horses, the wrecked incentives, peasants who would not work land that was no longer theirs — while quotas rose, and the machine met the shortfall as it had in 1920: as sabotage. What distinguishes 1932–33 from 1921, and the course states the distinction as the record's central fact, is that this famine's geography and instruments were administratively shaped. The August 7, 1932 law — peasants called it the "five ears of grain" law — made collective-farm property sacred and its theft, down to gleaning a harvested field, punishable by death or ten years. The blacklist system sealed indebted villages off from all trade. The November–December 1932 decrees stripped whole districts of the right to receive manufactured goods and demanded the return of grain "advances." The internal passport, introduced December 1932, was denied to peasants as a class, locking them to the land; and the directives of January 22, 1933 — signed by Stalin and Molotov — ordered the OGPU to prevent peasants from leaving Ukraine and the Kuban in search of bread, on the stated grounds that the exodus was organized by enemies. Roadblocks turned back the starving toward the villages where the grain, seed included, had been taken. Through the famine's first months the state remained a grain exporter — reduced volumes, but exports — and rejected the visible option of large-scale relief until spring 1933, when partial aid arrived with the planting season, calibrated less to the dying than to the next harvest.

The demography, in the course's discipline of ranges. Across the USSR, the famine of 1932–33 killed, in excess deaths, somewhere between five and nine million people, with the most careful recent reconstructions clustering toward the middle: on the order of 6–8 million. Ukraine bore the largest absolute share — the modern demographic studies converge near 3.5–3.9 million excess deaths, against older estimates that ran higher — and Kazakhstan the largest proportional one: roughly 1.5 million dead including about 1.3–1.4 million Kazakhs, a third or more of the Kazakh people, destroyed through the forced sedentarization of a pastoral nation whose herds were collectivized into extinction.[^1] The Kuban, the Volga, and the Don complete the map. The course fixes one comparative fact for L30's accounting: this was the deadliest single event in the ledger of European history between the wars, and it occurred in peacetime, in the empire's breadbasket, under a state that was simultaneously selling wheat abroad and photographing record harvests for the press.

Which introduces the accessory after the fact. The famine was contemporaneously denied — by the Soviet state, which barred correspondents from the region, and by the dean of the Moscow press corps, Walter Duranty of the *New York Times*, whose dispatches ridiculed famine reports as propaganda while he privately estimated millions dead to British diplomats; his 1932 Pulitzer stands unrevoked. Against him the record honors the Welsh journalist Gareth Jones, who walked the Kharkiv countryside and reported what he saw under his own name, and Malcolm Muggeridge, who smuggled dispatches out unsigned; both were vilified, and Jones was dead within two years. The episode is the course's first full case of a pattern Unit 6 will theorize — the Western intellectual as the regime's character witness — and it is why this unit trusts archives over contemporaries.

## The Genocide Question

Was it genocide? The course refuses to make the answer easy in either direction, because the scholarly dispute is real and instructive. The intentionalist case — associated with Robert Conquest's pioneering *The Harvest of Sorrow*, and pressed in different forms by Andrea Graziosi, Timothy Snyder, and Anne Applebaum — holds that whatever the famine's all-Union origins in collectivization, its lethal intensification in Ukraine and the Kuban from late 1932 was *directed*: the border sealings, the blacklists, the quota policy, and the simultaneous demolition of Ukrainian cultural and party cadres constitute a decision to break the Ukrainian peasantry as the base of Ukrainian nationhood — a reading in which the term Holodomor, "killing by hunger," carries its full weight, and one adopted as genocide by a growing list of parliaments. The structuralist reply — most rigorously in R. W. Davies and Stephen Wheatcroft's *The Years of Hunger* — finds no document ordering death by starvation and reads the catastrophe as the outcome of ruthless extraction, ideological blindness, and criminal indifference rather than a plan to exterminate: mass killing by policy, they insist, but not premeditated murder of a nation as such. The course's adjudication is deliberately narrow. First, the factual core is not in dispute between these camps — the decrees, the seized seed, the sealed borders, the exports, the denial are common ground; the argument is over intent's precise object. Second, on any reading the event satisfies the definition this unit actually needs: deaths in the millions produced by policy, foreseeable, foreseen in the event's course, and answered with concealment rather than correction. Third, the course notes that Raphael Lemkin — the jurist who coined "genocide" — himself described the destruction of the Ukrainian nation as a classic case, dimension by dimension; the term's author, at least, had no doubt where it applied. The reader may weigh the archives with Davies and Wheatcroft and still find, with them, a regime guilty of its people's deaths; or follow Applebaum and Snyder to the sterner verdict. What no reading permits is the verdict the regime demanded and Duranty delivered: that nothing happened.

## The Honest Counterpoint

The remaining defenses, stated at strength and audited. *The famine was Union-wide, not anti-Ukrainian* — partly sustained: Kazakhstan's proportional catastrophe and the Volga's dead are real, and the course has counted them; but the defense addresses the genocide debate's object, not the indictment's, and the Ukraine-specific instruments of December–January remain in the file. *The harvest genuinely failed* — sustained as far as it goes: 1932's crop was poor by any measure; but states meet poor harvests with imports, relief, and open roads, and this one chose exports, extraction, and cordons — the gap between failure and famine is exactly where the policy lived. *Industrialization required accumulation, and the war soon to come vindicated it* — the course gives this its due, for it is the strongest form: the tractors and the Urals steel of 1943 trace to the 1930s plans. But the audit finds the premise false on the regime's own archives — famine wrecked agriculture for a decade, the livestock subtraction alone outweighed the grain-export earnings, and economists' reconstructions find the peasant-squeeze contributed marginally to investment — and finds the moral arithmetic the course's entire subject: a state that buys steel with millions of its farmers has not answered the indictment; it has restated it. The truce Lenin signed with the peasantry, Stalin thus concluded in blood and called victory; in January 1933 he announced that the kolkhoz system had triumphed. The countryside broken, the machine turned inward — on the Party, the army, and the census-takers who had counted the missing. To the Great Terror.
